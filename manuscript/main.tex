\begin{abstract}
Can a large language model (LLM) revise material-level replenishment parameters effectively? We evaluate a prompt-configured LLM, without project-specific training, inside \emph{InventOR}: it proposes a reorder-point/order-quantity $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification; deterministic software checks and replays it; a planner retains decision authority. For 365 plant-material pairs from three plants of one manufacturer, three original LLM runs, seven reference policies and an ablation withholding ERP planning parameters are evaluated on held-out demand. Three further generations on a masked public release test replication, not six independent datasets. On the original 346-pair common-feasible cohort, the LLM costs about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters but 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile baselines. Policy summaries resemble textbook benchmark policies, with median smoothed safety-stock and order-quantity ratios of 1.00 and reorder-point ratio 1.09. The mean within-pair cost range across original runs is 10.5\% of pair mean cost. Code using ERP safety lead time costs 44\% more than the LLM. Rescoring fixed policies with lost sales priced at 1\% of unit cost or more largely compresses non-tuned cost differences. Unconstrained trajectories breach storage limits for 53--66\% of pairs; capacity-clipped replay can violate order-level MOQ. Masked runs preserve the principal ranking. InventOR supports checkable candidate generation, not demonstrated optimization superiority or improved planner outcomes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Replenishment parameters in manufacturing are set per plant-material pair: a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity. In large ERP systems these values often remain at levels set years earlier, while demand, supplier performance and storage constraints change. Revisiting them requires analysts who can fit demand and lead-time statistics, apply inventory theory, respect minimum order quantities (MOQs) and storage limits, and explain the result to planners. A conversational large language model (LLM) with a code interpreter appears to offer this scarce skill, and a growing literature explores such tools for supply-chain and operations decisions \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, trifone2026agentic}, building on the long-standing promise of data analytics in logistics \cite{wang2016bigdata}.

Three problems limit its use. First, LLM outputs can be unfaithful or fabricated \cite{ji2023survey}, so a proposed policy is a candidate, not a decision. Second, superiority claims over ERP parameters are hard to interpret when the LLM and the comparators receive different information. Third, a storage rule that holds when a policy is written down can be violated as stock builds up over time.

Prior LLM work includes solver-code generation, inventory-control agents and human--AI simulation optimization (Section~\ref{sec:literature}). Our focus is narrower: fixed per-material $(r,Q)$ proposals for an industrial portfolio, evaluated against seven reference arms, including ERP-derived parameters, with explicit input boundaries. We call the workflow \emph{InventOR}: for each plant-material pair, an enterprise LLM application receives a fixed prompt, with no project-specific training or fine-tuning, and proposes a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and escalation flag. Deterministic software parses, gates and simulates proposals; a planner retains authority over ERP changes (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

We study five questions on real industrial data: what cost, service and inventory the LLM policies achieve relative to ERP-derived parameters, data-driven baselines and textbook code, with explicit input boundaries (RQ1); how their differences relate descriptively to parameter similarity, run-to-run noise, demand segments and shortage prices (RQ2); whether policies respect storage limits along simulated trajectories (RQ3); how stable they are across repeated generations (RQ4); and which plant and demand-regime results might inform proposed review and escalation rules (RQ5).

We contribute (C1) a workflow with explicit requirements that bound the LLM's role (Sections~\ref{sec:scope} and \ref{sec:aid}); (C2) an evaluation with seven reference arms, including textbook formula-based code, an ablation withholding ERP planning parameters and hard-limit replay (Section~\ref{sec:design}); (C3) three original runs on 365 target pairs and three fresh generations on a masked version of the same panel, with a matched six-run report, cost decomposition, shortage-price break-even and descriptive mechanism analyses (Section~\ref{sec:results}); and (C4) implications for practice and research (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

Against ERP-derived parameters the original LLM policies cost about 11\% less at mean fill 0.1--0.4 points lower, but cost 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile policies at similar mean fill. This is not an equal-service experiment. Static-compliant policies exceed storage limits on unconstrained trajectories for 53--66\% of pairs. Smoothed median policy ratios resemble textbook code, with reorder points 9\% higher; these summaries do not reveal internal calculations. Code using ERP inputs costs 44--45\% more than the LLM, illustrating the importance of input treatment. Pricing lost sales at 1\% of unit cost or more brings five policy arms within 4\% of each other; the simulation-tuned arm is cheaper under these fixed-policy revaluations. The six-run matched report preserves the main ranking. Written rationales and flags are possible review aids, not measured planner benefits or development-effort savings.


\section{Problem Setting and Workflow}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{The decision problem}
\label{subsec:decision}
The decision supported is the periodic (re)setting of a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity for each plant-material pair held in an ERP system. It is a partly structured operational-control decision \cite{gorry1971framework}: the calculation is textbook, but the choice of estimator, the treatment of intermittent demand, the plausibility of recorded lead times and the handling of constraints require judgement that is applied inconsistently when thousands of materials compete for a few analysts' time. The decision-maker is the material planner; hard constraints are the minimum order quantity (MOQ) and a physical storage limit; and feedback is slow, because a changed parameter shows its effect only in later demand. The scope is one pair at a time (single item, single echelon, continuous review, lost sales, $(r,Q)$ family); order release, supplier selection, forecasting-model development and multi-echelon networks are out of scope. The LLM proposes candidates; it does not place orders, make deployment decisions or run the study's deterministic held-out evaluation replay.

\subsection{The InventOR workflow and its level of automation}
\label{subsec:definition}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Definition.} \emph{InventOR is a human-reviewed planning workflow that, for one plant-material pair at a time, (i) obtains from a large language model, configured only by a written prompt, a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; (ii) verifies the candidate deterministically against explicit constraints; (iii) evaluates it by counterfactual simulation against reference policies on held-out demand; and (iv) presents the result to a planner, who alone decides whether ERP parameters are changed.}
\end{quote}
In the levels of automation of \textcite{parasuraman2000model}, InventOR operates at a low-to-intermediate level: it suggests one alternative and leaves execution to the human. Accepting suggestions invites complacency and automation bias \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}, which the review rules of Section~\ref{subsec:rules} are meant to counter.

\label{subsec:requirements}
The requirements follow from the known unreliability of LLM outputs \cite{ji2023survey}, guidance on human--AI interaction and accountability \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, run-to-run nondeterminism of LLM inference \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism} and the constraints of inventory control. \section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Human--AI decision making in planning.}
Decision support aims to improve the judgement of a human decision-maker, not to replace it \cite{gorry1971framework, sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, and how suggestions are scoped, presented and verified determines whether users rely on them appropriately \cite{parasuraman2000model, parasuraman2010complacency}. In inventory control, human override of algorithmic safety-stock advice is a documented risk \cite{wang2024aversion}, people discount algorithms after seeing them err \cite{dietvorst2015aversion}, and AI-based route planning yields efficiency advantages when it is followed in full \cite{loske2021humanai}. InventOR therefore keeps the planner as decision-maker and makes the evidence for each proposal explicit.

\paragraph{LLMs in operations and supply-chain decisions.}
\textcite{wu2026disruptions} compares automated and human-specified LLM-assisted inventory optimization under supply-chain disruptions through numerical experiments. Our study instead freezes per-material policies for held-out replay on an industrial portfolio; it is not the first use of LLMs in inventory optimization.
Reviews and frameworks map the opportunities of generative AI in supply chains \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, li2024genaiscm, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai} and stress human roles, data quality and governance \cite{trifone2026agentic, cannas2023aiscm, venkatesh2023adoption}. Evidence from manufacturing planning is thin: a fine-tuned LLM for digital-manufacturing optimization \cite{amarasinghe2026finetuned}, a transformer trained to set order timing and quantity \cite{liu2026transformer} and evidence that ChatGPT reproduces managers' decision biases \cite{chen2025manager}. Most technical LLM work translates problem statements into solver code \cite{li2023large, huang2025orlm, ahmaditeshnizi2024optimus, zhang2025or} or studies multi-agent control in inventory flows \cite{quan2024invagent, jannelli2025agentic}. The closest conceptual neighbour studies how OR algorithms, LLM agents and humans complement each other in multi-period inventory control on a benchmark of more than 1,000 instances, and reports that OR-augmented LLM methods outperform either alone \cite{baek2026inventorybench}; we ask the prior question of how far an LLM alone gets on industrial data. In forecasting, LLMs do not consistently outperform human judgment in retail \cite{abolghasemi2025humans}, and an LLM forecasting copilot for spare-parts demand shows high variability for irregular demand that averaging repeated queries reduces \cite{nakhost2025copilot}. We test run-to-run variability directly (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}) and let the LLM propose a complete policy, not a forecast.

\paragraph{Data-driven inventory control without LLMs.}
Learning-based replenishment includes policies learned from non-stationary demand \cite{ren2024datadriven}, forecasts with leading indicators \cite{sagaert2025leading}, deep reinforcement learning for omnichannel and multi-echelon systems \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl, ma2026omnichannel}, contextual robust optimization \cite{wang2026contextual} and reinforcement learning combined with demand forecasting \cite{schett2026rlorder}; heuristics remain competitive in constrained settings \cite{gulecyuz2025heuristic}. A trained framework jointly sets replenishment parameters, safety stock and safety lead time on automotive data \cite{gabellini2026novel}. Forecasting research links forecasts to safety stock \cite{beutel2012causal}, combined-forecast error distributions \cite{barrow2016distributions} and inventory-cost objectives \cite{kourentzes2020optimising}. In \emph{Expert Systems with Applications}, model-based deep reinforcement learning addresses new products with short histories \cite{demizu2023inventory}, and contextual agents adapt to unseen supply-chain configurations \cite{batsis2024contextual}. TFT-MPIR directly generates multi-period replenishment orders from historical and contextual inputs \cite{guo2025tftmpir}; graph recurrent networks address store-SKU replenishment \cite{ma2024retail}; RevIN-TSMixer connects demand forecasting to continuous-review inventory costs \cite{nguyen2026timemixer}. InventOR instead evaluates a fixed-prompt, pretrained generator without project-specific model fitting; it does not establish superiority over these trained methods.

\paragraph{Intermittent and spare-parts demand.}
Manufacturing histories are often intermittent or lumpy. Work began with the bias of Croston's estimator and its stock-control performance \cite{syntetos2001bias, syntetos2006stock} and continues with neural \cite{kourentzes2013neural}, bootstrapping \cite{hasni2019adjusted}, obsolescence-aware \cite{babai2019obsolescence}, temporal-hierarchy \cite{sanguri2023reconciliation} and state-space \cite{svetunkov2023iets} forecasts, order-up-to control \cite{rostamitabar2023orderupto}, safety-stock placement \cite{yazdani2023safetystock} and lead-time forecasting \cite{zhang2026leadtime}, all of which bear on the inputs of the policies we compare. The wider literature adds specialized estimators and classification schemes \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy, willemain2004new, prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, spare-parts forecasting \cite{goldsmith2025spare} and multi-echelon spare-parts models \cite{caserta2026amazon}. InventOR is not a new inventory model and does not claim to beat trained or optimized methods; its $(r,Q)$ policy is the standard workhorse of continuous-review control \cite{hadley1963analysis, porteus2002foundations, silver2017inventory}.

\section{The InventOR Workflow}
\label{sec:aid}

\subsection{Architecture and components}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Following the classical decision-support components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, InventOR has a \emph{data} component (a material-scoped extract of daily demand, lead times, working-day flags, cost and constraint parameters and ERP reference values; the fields supplied are controlled and logged; DR4), a \emph{knowledge} component (the system prompt file defining the task, the prohibited methods, the storage rules and the output schema, Supplementary File S1; DR1, DR5), a \emph{model} component split into a generator (an enterprise LLM application with a code interpreter, configured only by the prompt, proposing one $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; DR1) and a deterministic verifier and simulator (parser, gate, held-out simulation with hard storage-limit replay and reference arms; DR2, DR3), a \emph{dialog} component (policy, justification, flag and simulated comparison shown to the planner under the trust, escalate and reject rules; DR6) and a \emph{governance} layer (frozen prompt file, per-call log of input hash, time, session and raw response, repeated runs; DR5). The generator is the only non-inspectable component. In the evaluated prototype the dialog component is the per-pair output record; no interactive interface was built or evaluated.

\subsection{Operation in four decision steps}
For one material, InventOR uses four decision steps after input preparation; Figure~\ref{fig:tool_process_map} includes preparation as a fifth box.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figure_1_Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{InventOR workflow: material data are prepared, an LLM proposes a policy, and deterministic software parses, checks and simulates it. Planner review is a proposed deployment step, not an evaluated activity.}
  \label{fig:tool_process_map}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Generate.} The LLM, accessed through an enterprise application with a code interpreter, receives one material's history (daily demand, realized lead times, working-day flags) and a short parameter block (unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target, storage limit, and current ERP safety stock and safety lead time for comparison). A written prompt (the only configuration; no weights are changed) \cite{sahoo2024systematic} requests transparent statistics, policy-family selection and a structured policy with a short justification and escalation flag. It gives an EOQ-related quantity rule with MOQ and storage bounds but does not prescribe the normal-approximation safety-stock formula used by the textbook comparators. All scored artifacts resolve to fixed $(r,Q)$ policies from their emitted parameters; permitted family labels are not separate evaluated methods.
\item \textbf{Gate.} Deterministic code extracts the policy, rejects malformed or infeasible outputs (missing or non-numeric fields, non-positive reorder point or order quantity, negative safety stock, reorder point below safety stock, safety stock plus order quantity above the storage limit) and retries the generation up to three times. The backtest applies one further admission check, that the order quantity is not below the MOQ.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Each accepted policy is replayed on the material's demand observed \emph{after} the data cutoff, a held-out evaluation in the sense of out-of-sample forecast testing \cite{tashman2000out, hewamalage2023forecast}, together with the comparator policies, producing holding and ordering cost, fill rate and stockout days.
\item \textbf{Review (proposed deployment step).} A planner would inspect the policy, justification, escalation flag and simulated comparison and would retain sole authority over ERP changes. Recording the planner's decision, review time and comments is proposed future functionality; neither this logging nor planner outcomes was evaluated in the present study.
\end{enumerate}

The LLM is a \emph{candidate generator} whose output is gated; it does not run the study's deterministic held-out evaluation, place orders or decide ERP changes. The prompt prohibits Monte Carlo simulation, distribution fitting, machine-learning libraries and specialized intermittent-demand estimators, so that the policy rests on transparent statistics.

\subsection{Trust, review and reject rules}
\label{subsec:rules}
We propose three rules for planners, in line with guidelines for human--AI interaction and AI auditing \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}. The rules and the 0.5-point threshold are design proposals not evaluated with planners; the trust rule would be met by a minority of pairs (the LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in 74--77 of 346).
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[Trust (consider after review)] only after checking both order-level MOQ and storage compliance, as well as a simulated cost no higher than the best history-only reference (Croston/SBA or empirical quantile) with fill not more than about 0.5 points lower. Capacity-clipped replay alone cannot establish the joint compliance needed to apply this rule.
\item[Escalate] when the LLM raises an escalation flag, the material is lumpy or has strongly seasonal or trending demand, the plant or calendar data were repaired, or the policy differs strongly between repeated generations.
\item[Reject] when the proposal fails the gate after the retry budget, exceeds the storage limit in the hard-limit replay, or is dominated (higher cost and lower fill) by a history-only reference.
\end{description}

\subsection{Provenance and reproducibility of the configuration}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The system prompt, a plain-text file deployed unchanged in the enterprise application, defines the task, forbidden methods, capacity rules and output schema (Supplementary File S1). Every call logs the SHA-256 of its input, UTC time, session identifier and raw response. The platform does not expose the model identifier or sampling settings; the evaluated model (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 with a code interpreter) is operator-declared. Reproducibility rests on the prompt file, the call logs and three independent runs (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}).

\section{Application and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Evaluation approach}
\label{subsec:evalapproach}
The evaluation addresses the requirements of Table~\ref{tab:requirements}. The primary basis is the hard-limit replay with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty; sensitivity analyses vary these choices. This is an ex post simulation study with real demand, simplified dynamics and no live users. The history-only, SLT-informed and ERP-floor OR arms implement textbook EOQ and normal-approximation policies, with different treatments of ERP inputs; they are reference designs, not controls for exact execution of a prescribed prompt formula. The experiment characterizes policy cost, service and constraint failures, not usability, adoption or improved planner decisions.

\subsection{Industrial application and data}\label{subsec:data}
The data come from a large manufacturer, and the evaluation is retrospective. The dataset contains 233{,}078 daily records for 411 plant-material pairs from three plants (A, B and C), with a data cutoff on 10 March 2026 and a post-cutoff horizon observed to 27 August 2026. Eligibility requires at least 20 working-day observations before the cutoff and 40 on or after it within the evaluation horizon. Of the 411 pairs, 365 are eligible for scoring (234 in Plant A, 65 in Plant B, 66 in Plant C) and are sent to the LLM. For 19 pairs (all in Plant A), at least one of the SAP-derived or SLT-informed reference policies cannot be projected to satisfy MOQ and the static $SS+Q$ capacity rule while retaining its safety stock; these are excluded from outcome comparison, leaving a \emph{common-feasible cohort} of 346 pairs (215, 65 and 66). This is a comparator-specific exclusion, not proof of general policy infeasibility. Demand is concentrated (the five largest materials account for 40.3\% of demand). The source working-day flag of Plant C is all zero; we reconstruct it from the same-date majority of Plants A and B and test this repair in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}. Data quality is a known threat to decision support \cite{watts2009data} and inventory records \cite{dehoratius2008inventory, kang2005information, mersereau2013information}.

Deterministic scoring normalizes dates and source-field aliases, sums duplicate material-date demand before taking its absolute value, averages positive non-empty lead times within each material-date and omits empty or non-positive lead times from statistics. The implementation is in \texttt{inventory\_llm/core.py}: \texttt{load\_grouped\_rows}, \texttt{demand}, \texttt{compute\_stats} and \texttt{eligible\_item\_keys}.

Each pair is sent as a material-scoped CSV with daily demand, recorded lead times, a working-day flag, the minimum order quantity, unit cost, holding-cost rate, ordering cost, service-level target, the storage limit, and two \emph{ERP planning parameters} (current safety stock and safety lead time), which are withheld in the ablation arm. The CSV also carries supplier and lane context fields that the prompt does not use. A masked version of the panel is released for replication (Section~\ref{subsec:masked}); there the plants are labelled PL1, PL2 and PL3 (corresponding to A, B and C).

The LLM and the classical comparators therefore do not necessarily use the same information, and we construct arms that make it explicit (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

Three further arms use no ERP planning inputs. \emph{Croston/SBA OR} uses bias-corrected Croston demand \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy}, smoothing 0.1, and root mean squared one-step forecast error as demand spread, a study-specific heuristic rather than a validated lead-time-demand distribution. \emph{Empirical-quantile OR} uses the service-target quantile of rolling demand over rounded mean lead time. \emph{Simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$} searches reserve factors $-0.5,-0.25,\ldots,3.5$ and quantity multiples $0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2$ (up to 85 candidates), selecting the cheapest meeting the service target, or the best fill if none meets it. Statistics use all pre-cutoff data; tuning replays the last 250 working days without capacity clipping, initialized at each candidate's $R+Q$ with an empty pipeline. This is in-sample tuning, not nested validation; evaluation instead uses shared observed opening stock and capacity clipping. No post-cutoff demand informs selection. Deep reinforcement learning \cite{stranieri2024drl, kolyaei2025rl} is not benchmarked.

\subsection{Simulation, capacity replay and metrics}
Each pair is replayed as a single-item, single-echelon $(r,Q)$ system \cite{hadley1963analysis, axsater2015inventory, zipkin2000foundations, silver2017inventory, porteus2002foundations} with lost sales \cite{bijvank2011lost}, a discrete working-day approximation to continuous review, shared observed opening stock, an empty pipeline and deterministic arrivals after the rounded mean lead time (\ref{app:math}). This is a simulation experiment, not a reconstruction of actual orders \cite{grasso1984simulation}. The policy quantity satisfies the supplier MOQ \cite{heese2025moq}, but the hard-limit replay prioritizes capacity \cite{zhang2023replenishment}: clipped orders may fall below MOQ. Metrics are mean material-horizon holding-plus-ordering cost, mean material-level and volume-weighted fill rates, and stockout days. Paired 10{,}000-resample bootstrap 95\% intervals describe this cohort; material dependence within three plants precludes population-level inference.

A static check $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$ does not show that storage limits hold as inventory evolves, because the reorder point includes lead-time demand and inventory on order can exceed the limit on arrival. We therefore record the post-receipt on-hand stock for every policy and day and report the share of pairs and days above the limit (the \emph{unconstrained} simulation), and we \emph{replay} every policy with each order truncated to the storage limit minus the current inventory position (the \emph{hard-limit replay}), which is our primary evaluation.

\subsection{Repeated runs and sensitivities}
To examine run-to-run variation, the entire cohort is generated three times (runs 1, 2 and 3) with the same prompt file and the same data. Run~1 serves as the reference for tabulated results unless stated otherwise; runs 2 and 3 provide replicates. Sensitivity analyses vary the working-day reconstruction (majority, any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals), the shortage penalty (zero, one and five times unit cost).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Completion and policy characteristics}\label{subsec:completion}
All four runs (three with the full input and one ablation, Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) of the original data return a valid, storage-feasible $(r,Q)$ policy for all 365 pairs after at most three attempts per pair. The validity gate rejects non-positive reorder points and order quantities, negative safety stocks and reorder points below the safety stock. The ERP safety stock is zero for 198 of the 365 pairs (188 of the 346 in the cohort below), so for a majority of pairs the SAP-derived arm holds no safety stock at all.

\subsection{Headline comparison with ERP-derived parameters}\label{subsec:headline}
Unless stated otherwise, all results use the hard-limit replay on the 346-pair common-feasible cohort with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty (Section~\ref{subsec:capacity} gives the unconstrained simulation). Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results} compares all arms; its last columns give the LLM cost relative to each arm in the three runs (positive: the LLM costs more). In run~1 the LLM policies cost 11.4\% less than SAP-derived policies and 30.5\% less than the SLT-informed OR (mean 2{,}928 versus 3{,}305 and 4{,}211), at a mean fill 0.18 points lower than SAP-derived (98.11\% versus 98.30\%; interval $-0.54$ to $+0.10$). The mean cost difference against SAP-derived is $-377$ (95\% interval $-651$ to $-154$); the LLM is cheaper in 241 of 346 pairs and, in 226, simultaneously no more expensive and no worse in fill. It incurs more stockout days (910 versus 804). The volume-weighted (aggregate) fill rate is only 78.7--79.3\% for every arm except the simulation-tuned policy (84.8\%), against a mean material-level fill of about 98\%: with lost sales unpriced, service on high-volume materials is low under all policies, so the cost ranking is not a comparison at equal service.

Relative to SAP-derived parameters the LLM carries about 17\% less average inventory (16{,}256 versus 19{,}565 units) and about 16\% lower holding cost at about the same number of orders per year (17.3 versus 17.6), and it accepts 11--20\% more stockout days (runs 3 and 2). The saving is therefore a trade of inventory for service, not a better ordering rhythm, and Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that once shortages carry a cost this trade no longer favours the LLM.

\subsection{Information asymmetry: what the LLM adds beyond the ERP inputs}
\label{subsec:arms-results}

First, the SLT-informed OR and its ERP-floor variant are the most expensive arms (4{,}211 and 4{,}248): the ERP safety lead time inflates their effective lead time and buffers. Against them the LLM is 30--31\% cheaper in every run, which mostly measures how heavily ERP inputs over-buffer.

Second, the \emph{history-only OR}, which uses no ERP inputs, costs 2{,}764 at a fill of 98.14\%. The LLM costs 5.9--6.0\% more in all three runs (run~1: $+164$, interval $+91$ to $+240$), with fill differences of $-0.03$, $-0.25$ and $+0.01$ points whose intervals include zero, and is cheaper in only 74--77 of 346 pairs. The stronger data-driven arms widen the gap: the LLM costs 8.2--8.4\% more than the Croston/SBA OR (2{,}705, fill 98.30\%; run~1: $+223$, interval $+97$ to $+362$; cheaper in 91--99 pairs) and 17.7--17.8\% more than the empirical-quantile OR (2{,}489, fill 98.35\%; $+439$, interval $+329$ to $+557$; cheaper in 44--49 pairs; mean fill 0.24 points lower). Part of the LLM's advantage over ERP-derived parameters therefore reflects weak ERP benchmarks.

The simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$ policy sits at a different operating point: an aggregate fill of 84.8\% and the lowest inventory (11{,}914 units) but a mean fill of 96.05\% and 1{,}973 stockout days, because it is tuned on the pre-cutoff year. The LLM costs 10.3--10.4\% less at zero penalty with a 2.1-point higher mean fill, but the interval includes zero ($-2{,}527$ to $+857$); once shortages are priced the tuned policy is by far the cheapest arm (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{LLM without ERP planning parameters (ablation)}
\label{subsec:ablation}
This ablation blanks ERP safety stock and safety lead time, marking them ``not supplied'', with the same prompt and other inputs. All 365 original pairs returned a static-compliant policy. Median parameter ratios to run~1 are 1.00, but reorder points differ by more than 10\% in 54.6\% of pairs, against 48.3\% and 48.0\% between full-input runs. Hard-limit cost is 3{,}014 at mean fill 98.06\%: 8.8\% below SAP-derived (interval $-566$ to $-61$), but 9.0\% above History-only (interval $+134$ to $+387$), 11.4\% above Croston/SBA and 21.1\% above empirical-quantile. Relative to SAP-derived, costs are $-10.8\%$, $-21.9\%$ and $+41.6\%$ in Plants A/B/C. The Plant C reversal therefore persists without these fields; one stochastic ablation does not isolate their causal effect or prove that the LLM ignores them. Costs, MOQ, service target and capacity remain available to all arms.

\subsection{Storage limits along simulated trajectories}
\label{subsec:capacity}
All 365 original LLM policies satisfy $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$, yet unconstrained post-receipt stock exceeds the limit for 183 pairs (52.9\%; 178--183 across runs; 10.2\% of pair-days) for the LLM, 198 (57.2\%) for SAP-derived and 230 (66.5\%) for SLT-informed OR. The static rule does not bound pipeline accumulation and is insufficient for trajectory feasibility. In the hard-limit replay no arm exceeds the limit by more than one unit (integer truncation of inventory position); this is not simultaneous proof of hard MOQ compliance. Enforcing capacity raises LLM mean cost from 2{,}850 to 2{,}928 and SAP-derived from 3{,}184 to 3{,}305 without changing the ranking.

\subsection{Plants and demand regimes}
\label{subsec:plants}
The average hides a strong plant effect. In original run~1 the LLM costs 14.1\% less than SAP-derived in Plant A (cheaper in 179 of 215 pairs) and 22.7\% less in Plant B (54 of 65), but 36.2\% \emph{more} in Plant C (cheaper in 8 of 66); the Plant~C loss is similar in the other full runs ($+32.2\%$, $+31.5\%$) and persists under every working-day reconstruction in which Plant C can be scored (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}). Figure~\ref{fig:segments} separately shows all six runs on the matched masked-scale cohort. Against the data-driven baselines the original run~1 LLM costs more in every plant: 7.2\%, 3.6\% and 6.7\% more than the history-only OR, 13.1\%, 2.3\% and 6.2\% more than the Croston/SBA OR, and 20.8\%, 7.7\% and 29.5\% more than the empirical-quantile OR in Plants A, B and C. The SLT-informed and history-only ORs coincide in Plant C because ERP safety lead time is zero for all its pairs.

By demand regime, a study-specific high-variability proxy (pre-cutoff working-day demand coefficient of variation $\mathrm{CV}\geq1$) identifies 33 materials in the 346-pair original cohort. Labelled ``lumpy'' in the evidence code, this is not the conventional ADI/$\mathrm{CV}^2$ taxonomy. They show the largest reduction against SAP-derived ($-25.4\%$ in run~1), but the history-only and Croston/SBA ORs cost about 9--10\% less than the LLM there; with 33 materials this is indicative only.

\subsection{Descriptive comparison with formula-based code}\label{subsec:mechanism}
The deterministic History-only OR uses textbook EOQ with an MOQ floor and normal-approximation safety stock (\ref{app:math}); the SLT-informed and ERP-floor ORs additionally use ERP inputs available to the LLM (Section~\ref{sec:design}). The historical prompt constrains the economic quantity and permits several policy families; it does not prescribe these comparator formulas in full. This is a descriptive comparison with reference code, not a test of compliance with a prescribed normal-approximation recipe.

\paragraph{The LLM resembles the formula-based policies.}
The median smoothed ratio $(x_{\mathrm{LLM}}+1)/(x_{\mathrm{code}}+1)$ is 1.00 for order quantity (medians 2{,}810 and 2{,}828 units) and safety stock (712 and 718, against an ERP median of 0); LLM parameters are averaged over the three original runs first. Here ``code'' denotes History-only OR. The corresponding reorder-point ratio is 1.09, including across the segments in Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}, where LLM cost is 4--9\% higher. These summaries indicate similarity and a reorder-point uplift, not item-level proof that the LLM executes the formulas faithfully.

\paragraph{Input treatment changes the ranking against code.}
Code that receives the same ERP inputs as the LLM costs 4{,}211 (SLT-informed OR) and 4{,}248 (ERP-floor OR) per pair against 2{,}928 for the LLM in run~1, i.e.\ 44--45\% more than the LLM, because the ERP safety lead times inflate the textbook reorder point. Code that ignores the ERP planning inputs costs 2{,}764; the LLM costs 5.9\% more than this code. The LLM resembles the History-only code rather than the ERP-inflated code, which is consistent with the ablation in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Run-to-run noise.}
The within-pair cost range across the three original runs, divided by that pair's mean and averaged across pairs, is 10.5\%; this is not a standard deviation. The runs disagree on beating History-only code for 34.1\% of pairs. The LLM mean is cheaper than History-only for 17.1\% of pairs and SAP-derived for 76.3\%. Selecting each pair's best run with hindsight gives mean cost 2{,}765 (History-only: 2{,}764); selecting the worst gives 3{,}146. Such selection is not an implementable out-of-sample policy.

\paragraph{Plant C and segments.}
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} shows that the LLM advantage over SAP-derived parameters disappears in storage-bound pairs ($+4.9\%$ against $-14.9\%$ elsewhere) and reverses in Plant C ($+33.3\%$); it does not vary monotonically with demand variability. Plant C is not an LLM-specific failure: the History-only code is also 28\% more costly than SAP-derived parameters there, so the ERP parameters are leaner than any formula-based policy in that plant when lost sales are not charged. Plant C also has the least regular demand (median coefficient of variation of daily demand 0.61, lead-time coefficient of variation 0.78, against 0.51 and 0.63 over all pairs).

\paragraph{Plant C calendar.} Plant C's working-day flag required reconstruction, so we re-scored all policies under four alternatives (any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C, calendar-day arrivals). Where Plant C can be scored, the LLM remains 31--35\% more expensive than SAP-derived there, so the Plant C result is not an artefact of the repair.

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Research Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
A prompt-configured LLM generates checkable $(r,Q)$ candidates: all original proposals passed the static gate, but masked generations include missing and invalid outputs. On scored cohorts they cost less than ERP-derived parameters and more than three history-based policies at zero shortage penalty. Policy similarity, a reorder-point uplift and run-to-run variation help characterize this gap; they do not establish the LLM's internal calculations or an inherent inability to outperform code. Information-matched inputs need not imply identical use of those inputs. InventOR is a reviewed candidate generator, not evidence of optimization superiority. Written rationales and escalation flags could aid review, but development effort, explanation quality, planner time and realized benefits were not measured.

\paragraph{Implications for practice.}
(1) Benchmark against data-driven code, not ERP values alone; zero safety stocks and large safety lead times make input treatment important. (2) Price shortages before interpreting cost rankings, which otherwise omit service losses (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}). (3) Verify trajectory storage and order-level MOQ jointly: static-compliant policies exceeded capacity, while clipped replay can violate MOQ. (4) Expect plant differences and define review and escalation rules before use \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}. (5) Control prompt versions, retain call provenance and repeat generations. (6) Generation took minutes per material rather than the much faster formula calculations; no controlled development-effort or planner-time study was conducted. Periodic regeneration is a possible planning use \cite{sengupta2025uncertainty}, not a demonstrated operational benefit.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) One manufacturer and three plants limit generalization; six generations reuse one underlying panel, not six independent datasets. (ii) Model identity is operator-declared; runtime identifier and sampling settings are unavailable. (iii) No planner outcomes or explanation-quality measures were collected. (iv) Deterministic mean-lead-time arrivals, empty starting pipelines and ignored observed receipts simplify operations; daily order review and capacity clipping can violate supplier MOQ. (v) Zero shortage penalty is the primary basis, not an equal-service comparison. Positive penalties rescore fixed trajectories rather than re-optimize policies. Demand concentration and within-plant dependence limit bootstrap interpretation. (vi) Only single-item, single-echelon $(r,Q)$ policies are compared. (vii) No trained neural or reinforcement-learning baseline is included; the tuned arm's in-sample initialization differs from evaluation. ERP-derived policies use ERP safety stock and safety lead time with our order quantity, not observed practice. (viii) The public masked panel and frozen policies support offline scoring, not exact regeneration of confidential original results or independent reproduction of proprietary-model inference. Per-pair masking changes portfolio weights and rounded quantities.

\paragraph{Research perspectives.}
(1) \emph{Ensembling and hybrids:} aggregating several generations may reduce individual-policy noise \cite{nakhost2025copilot}, and combining the best data-driven policy with LLM review \cite{wasserkrug2025enhancing} could capture both the OR cost level and the LLM's flags. (2) \emph{Comparison with trained methods:} benchmarks against trained inventory-control frameworks \cite{gabellini2026novel} and reinforcement-learning policies \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl}, as well as richer policy families such as $(s,S)$ \cite{prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, on identical data would clarify the trade-off between setup effort and policy quality. (3) \emph{Planner-in-the-loop field trials:} prospective studies should compare planners with and without the aid on matched materials, measuring planner time, override rates and realized service and inventory, and examine adoption drivers \cite{venkatesh2023adoption, cannas2023aiscm, trifone2026agentic}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On 365 industrial plant-material pairs, three original generations and three masked-release generations show that a prompt-configured LLM can propose replenishment parameters without project-specific training, but not invariably without invalid or missing outputs. In the original common-feasible cohort it costs about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at slightly lower mean service, yet 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile policies. Masked generations preserve this ranking, not independent external validity. Pricing shortages changes the ordering of fixed policies; static storage checks do not ensure trajectory feasibility, and capacity-clipped orders need not satisfy MOQ. Parameter similarity to textbook code is descriptive, not proof of internal formula fidelity. InventOR is supported as a checkable candidate generator inside a proposed reviewed process, not as an optimizer that beats data-driven baselines. Prospective planner studies and evaluation under simultaneous order-size and capacity constraints remain necessary.

\section{Formal Definitions}
\label{app:math}

\paragraph{Reference calculation.}
These are standard textbook calculations \cite{silver2017inventory, axsater2015inventory}. For pair $i$, $\bar d_i,\sigma_{D,i}$ describe pre-cutoff working-day demand; $L_i,\sigma_{L,i}$ describe recorded lead times; $K_i$ is ordering cost, $h_i$ annual holding cost per unit, and $z_i$ the normal service-target quantile. Set $Q_i=\max\{\mathrm{MOQ}_i,\lceil\sqrt{2K_i\cdot250\bar d_i/h_i}\,\rceil\}$, $\sigma_{LTD,i}=\sqrt{L_i^{\mathrm{eff}}\sigma_{D,i}^2+\bar d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2}$, $SS_i=\lceil z_i\sigma_{LTD,i}\rceil$ and $R_i=\lceil\bar d_iL_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+SS_i\rceil$. Here $L_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=L_i$ for History-only and $L_i+\max(\mathrm{ERP\ SLT}_i,0)$ for SLT-informed and ERP-floor; the latter also floors $SS_i$ at ERP safety stock. SAP-derived uses ERP safety stock, the same $Q_i$, and ERP safety lead time in its reorder point. Projection enforces $SS_i+Q_i\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ when MOQ plus safety stock fits.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
On each post-cutoff working day, due receipts arrive first, demand is served, unmet demand is lost, and at most one order is placed when inventory position (on hand plus on order) is at or below $R_i$. It arrives after the rounded mean lead time in subsequent working observations. Observed receipts, corrections and scrap are ignored. Cost is $TC_i=h_i\bar I_iT/250+K_iN_i$, where $\bar I_i$ is mean on-hand stock over $T$ working days and $N_i$ counts orders. Fill is served divided by demanded units. Hard-limit orders are $\min\{Q_i,\mathrm{capacity}_i-\lfloor\mathrm{position}_i\rfloor\}$ if positive, even when below MOQ; no order is placed otherwise. Observed opening stock is not clipped.
