\begin{abstract}
Material planners in manufacturing set reorder points, safety stocks and order quantities for thousands of materials, usually with ERP parameters that were last revised long ago. We present \emph{InventOR}, a human-reviewed decision aid in which a large language model (LLM), configured only through a prompt and without any training or fine-tuning, reads one material's demand and lead-time history and proposes a reorder-point/order-quantity $(r,Q)$ policy. Deterministic software parses every proposal, checks it against minimum order quantities and storage limits, and replays it on held-out post-cutoff demand before a planner sees it. We evaluate the aid on 365 plant-material pairs from three plants of an industrial manufacturer (346 in the common-feasible cohort), with three independent LLM runs. Against ERP-derived parameters, the LLM policies cost about 11\% less in the simulation at a service level 0.1--0.4 percentage points lower; against an operations-research (OR) policy that uses the same ERP inputs the cost is about 30\% lower. However, an OR policy computed from the history alone costs about 6\% \emph{less} than the LLM at the same service, so much of the apparent gain reflects the weakness of the ERP benchmarks rather than an optimization advantage of the LLM; the saving comes from holding about 17\% less inventory, at about 13\% more stockout days. When shortages are priced at one unit cost or more, the ERP-derived parameters become the cheapest arm and the LLM is about 2.4\% more expensive than them. When the ERP planning parameters are withheld from the LLM in a 365-pair ablation, it stays about 9\% cheaper than ERP-derived parameters and about 9\% more expensive than the history-only OR. Storage limits that appear satisfied in a static check are exceeded on 53--66\% of pairs during unconstrained simulated trajectories, depending on the method; a hard-limit replay is therefore our primary evaluation, and it leaves the ranking unchanged. At one plant the LLM is 31--36\% more expensive than ERP-derived parameters in the three full runs, a result that is robust to our calendar repairs. We derive rules for when such a tool should be trusted, reviewed or rejected, and set out a research agenda.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Replenishment parameters in manufacturing are set per plant-material pair: a reorder point that triggers an order, a safety stock that absorbs demand and lead-time variability, and an order quantity. In large ERP systems these values often remain at levels set years earlier, while demand patterns, supplier performance and storage constraints change. Revisiting them materially requires analysts who can fit demand and lead-time statistics, apply inventory theory, respect minimum order quantities (MOQs) and storage limits, and explain the result to planners. That scarce skill is exactly what a conversational large language model (LLM) with a code interpreter appears to offer, and a rapidly growing literature explores such tools for supply-chain and operations decisions \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, trifone2026agentic}.

The promise is real, but so are three problems that limit its use in practice. First, LLM outputs can be unfaithful or fabricated \cite{ji2023survey}, so a proposed policy must be treated as a candidate and not a decision. Second, claims of superiority over ERP parameters are hard to interpret when the LLM and the comparators are given different information; an LLM that sees a plant's ERP reference values and is compared with an OR policy that does not, or the reverse, tells us little about the LLM. Third, inventory policies live inside physical limits: a storage rule that holds when the policy is written down can be violated as stock builds up over time.

This paper addresses all three in an industrial application. We describe and evaluate \emph{InventOR}, a decision aid built around an enterprise LLM application that is configured with a prompt and receives no project-specific training or fine-tuning. The LLM proposes one $(r,Q)$ policy per plant-material pair; deterministic software parses, checks and simulates each proposal; a planner reviews proposals and simulation results before any ERP parameter is changed. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A human-reviewed decision-aid design} (Section~\ref{sec:aid}) that separates generation, deterministic gating, simulation and planner review, and states explicit rules for when its output is trusted, escalated or rejected.
\item \textbf{An information-controlled evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:design}) with four reference arms that differ in the information they use and, partly, in method, including an OR policy computed from history alone and an LLM ablation that is denied the ERP planning parameters. This separates what the LLM adds from what it is given.
\item \textbf{Trajectory-level storage verification}: every policy is replayed with orders truncated at the storage limit, and the replay, not the static parameter check, is the primary result.
\item \textbf{Complete-cohort evidence from three LLM runs on 365 pairs}, with plant-level, regime-level and sensitivity analyses, and managerial and research implications.
\end{enumerate}

Our main findings are as follows. Against ERP-derived parameters, the LLM policies are about 11\% cheaper at a service level that is 0.1--0.4 points lower. That advantage largely disappears when a reasonable OR baseline is available: a history-only $(r,Q)$ policy is about 6\% cheaper than the LLM at essentially equal service, and the LLM's saving over ERP parameters comes from carrying about 17\% less inventory at about 13\% more stockout days; once shortages are priced at one unit cost or more, the ERP-derived parameters are the cheapest arm. The LLM's value is therefore not superior optimization but the ability to produce a reviewable, auditable policy for every material without writing code. Storage limits are violated along unconstrained simulated trajectories for 53--66\% of pairs under every method, which makes trajectory checks a prerequisite for any replenishment decision aid. At one plant the LLM is 31--36\% more expensive than ERP-derived parameters, which we use to derive guidance on where to trust and where to review.

Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the data and the evaluation design. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results. Section~\ref{sec:managerial} derives managerial insights, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations and research directions. Formal definitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:math}.

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{LLMs in operations and supply-chain decisions.}
Reviews and frameworks map the opportunities for generative AI in supply chains \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, boone2025generative} and stress that adoption depends on assigned human roles, data quality and governance \cite{trifone2026agentic, cannas2023aiscm, venkatesh2023adoption, cohen2026supply}. A recent conceptual line proposes agentic digital twins that combine AI agents with model-based simulation and optimization to support decisions \cite{ivanov2026agentic}, and an LLM-plus-digital-twin method has been built for closed-loop supply-chain operations \cite{le2026llmdt}. Our decision aid follows the same logic at a smaller scale: an LLM proposes and a simulation model checks. Most technical LLM work focuses on translating natural-language problem statements into optimization models and solver code \cite{li2023large, huang2025orlm, ahmaditeshnizi2024optimus, zhang2025or}, on multi-agent control and consensus-seeking in inventory flows \cite{quan2024invagent, jannelli2025agentic}, on extracting supply-network structure from text \cite{almahri2025kgllm, zheng2025visibility}, on disruption modelling \cite{lin2025unisone}, or on evaluating LLMs on classical operations-research tasks such as vehicle routing \cite{hamdan2026routing}. The closest LLM study to ours uses an LLM as a forecasting copilot for spare-parts demand, combined with simulation, and reports high variability for irregular demand that can be reduced by averaging repeated queries and enriching prompts \cite{nakhost2025copilot}. We share the concern about run-to-run variability and test it directly (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}), but differ in that the LLM proposes a complete replenishment policy rather than a forecast.

\paragraph{Data-driven inventory control without LLMs.}
Learning-based replenishment is well established. Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to omnichannel and multi-echelon systems \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl} and aerospace replenishment \cite{wang2022aerospace}; deep-learning forecasting has been integrated with replenishment \cite{ma2026integrated}; contextual distributionally robust optimization uses side information to set order quantities \cite{wang2026contextual}; and distribution-free online algorithms handle capacitated warehouses under uncertain price and demand \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. Robust inventory control under demand and lead-time uncertainty has a longer history \cite{bertsimas2006robust, thorsen2017robust, zhang2025robust}, and lead-time variability and dependence are known to propagate through ordering decisions \cite{disney2025bullwhip}, while safety stock and safety lead time are alternative buffers against unreliability \cite{vanKampen2010safety}. The closest operational neighbour is a trained data-driven framework that jointly sets replenishment parameters, safety stock and safety lead time on real automotive data \cite{gabellini2026novel}. These methods require model training, historical scenario generation or tuned hyperparameters for each setting; InventOR requires none, at the price of an LLM-based generator whose internal logic cannot be inspected beyond its written justification.

\paragraph{Intermittent and spare-parts demand.}
Material histories in manufacturing are frequently intermittent or lumpy. The statistical literature offers specialized estimators and classification schemes \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy, willemain2004new, prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, and recent studies develop feature-based forecast combinations and temporal aggregation for intermittent demand \cite{sanguri2023intermittent, li2022featurebased}, forecasting for spare parts across product life-cycle phases \cite{goldsmith2025spare}, and integrated forecasting and planning or multi-echelon stocking models for repairable and spare parts \cite{babaveisi2022repairable, caserta2026amazon}. Our regime analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:plants}) shows where an LLM that is forbidden from using specialized intermittent-demand estimators behaves differently on lumpy materials.

Table~\ref{tab:literature-positioning} summarizes these streams and states how the contribution of InventOR differs. InventOR is not a new inventory model and does not claim to beat trained or optimized methods. It contributes (i) a way of using an off-the-shelf LLM as a bounded policy generator inside a reviewed process, (ii) an evaluation that controls the information available to each arm, and (iii) industrial evidence on cost, service and storage feasibility. The $(r,Q)$ policy it uses is the standard workhorse of continuous-review control \cite{hadley1963analysis, porteus2002foundations, silver2017inventory}.

\section{The InventOR Decision Aid}
\label{sec:aid}

\subsection{What the tool does, in plain terms}
A planner's question is: \emph{given what this material has done in the past, how much safety stock should I hold, at what stock level should I reorder, and how much should I order?} InventOR answers this question in four steps (Figure~\ref{fig:tool_process_map}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{Figure_1_Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{InventOR workflow: material data are prepared, an LLM proposes a policy, deterministic software parses, checks and simulates it, and a planner reviews the result.}
  \label{fig:tool_process_map}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Generate.} The LLM, accessed through an enterprise application with a code interpreter, receives one material's history (daily demand, realized lead times, working-day flags) and a short parameter block (unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target, storage limit, and current ERP safety stock and safety lead time for comparison). A written prompt (the only configuration; no weights are changed) \cite{sahoo2024systematic} instructs it to compute demand and lead-time statistics, classify the demand pattern, apply an economic-order-quantity (EOQ) argument and a normal-approximation safety stock, respect the storage limit, and report a structured policy together with a short justification and escalation flag.
\item \textbf{Gate.} Deterministic code extracts the policy, rejects malformed or infeasible outputs (missing or non-numeric fields, non-positive reorder point or order quantity, negative safety stock, reorder point below safety stock, safety stock plus order quantity above the storage limit) and retries the generation up to three times. The backtest applies one further admission check, that the order quantity is not below the MOQ.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Each accepted policy is replayed on the material's demand observed \emph{after} the data cutoff, a held-out evaluation in the sense of out-of-sample forecast testing \cite{tashman2000out, hewamalage2023forecast}, together with the comparator policies, producing holding and ordering cost, fill rate and stockout days. This plays the role that model-based simulation plays in digital-twin decision support \cite{ivanov2026agentic}, at the scale of a single policy.
\item \textbf{Review.} A planner sees the policy, the LLM's justification and flag, and the simulated comparison. Only the planner changes ERP parameters.
\end{enumerate}

The role of the LLM is deliberately narrow. It is a \emph{candidate generator} whose output is never trusted without the gate, and it is not used to forecast, to place orders, or to run the simulation. The prompt prohibits Monte Carlo simulation, distribution fitting, machine-learning libraries and specialized intermittent-demand estimators, so that the policy rests on transparent statistics a planner can reproduce.

\subsection{Trust, review and reject rules}
\label{subsec:rules}
We propose three explicit rules for planners, in line with guidelines for human--AI interaction and with internal-auditing practice for AI systems \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, and motivated by the evidence in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[Trust (apply after review)] when the proposal passes the gate, the simulated trajectory respects the storage limit when orders are truncated at it, and the simulated cost is not above that of a history-only OR reference with a fill rate not more than about 0.5 points lower.
\item[Escalate] when the LLM raises an escalation flag, the material is lumpy or has strongly seasonal or trending demand, the plant or calendar data were repaired, or the policy differs strongly between repeated generations.
\item[Reject] when the proposal fails the gate after the retry budget, exceeds the storage limit in the hard-limit replay, or is dominated (higher cost and lower fill) by the history-only reference.
\end{description}

\subsection{Provenance and reproducibility of the configuration}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The system prompt, which fully defines the task, the forbidden methods, the capacity rules and the output schema, is a plain-text file that is deployed unchanged in the enterprise application; it is distributed with the paper (Supplementary File S1) and the code repository. Every call logs the SHA-256 of its input data, the UTC time, the application session identifier and the full raw response. The platform does not expose the underlying model identifier or sampling settings to the client; the evaluated model (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6, with a code interpreter) is therefore declared by the operator and is not machine-verifiable. We report this limitation openly and base the reproducibility argument on the prompt file, the per-call logs and three independent runs (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}) rather than on a model checkpoint.

\section{Application and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Industrial application and data}
The application is a pilot at a large manufacturer. The dataset contains 233{,}078 daily records for 411 plant-material pairs from three plants (A, B and C), with a data cutoff on 10 March 2026 and a post-cutoff horizon observed to 27 August 2026. Of the 411 pairs, 365 are eligible for scoring (234 in Plant A, 65 in Plant B, 66 in Plant C) and are sent to the LLM. For 19 pairs (all in Plant A) the supplied MOQ plus the reference safety stock exceeds the storage limit, so no comparator can satisfy both rules; these are excluded from outcome comparison, leaving a \emph{common-feasible cohort} of 346 pairs (215, 65 and 66). All 365 pairs receive an LLM policy. Demand is concentrated: the five largest materials account for 40.3\% of demand and the ten largest for 57.8\%. The source working-day flag of Plant C is all zero; we reconstruct it from the same-date majority of Plants A and B (ties count as working days) and test the effect of this repair in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}. Such data-quality problems are a known threat to analytics-based decision support \cite{watts2009data} and, for inventory systems, to record accuracy \cite{dehoratius2008inventory, kang2005information, mersereau2013information}.

\subsection{Reference arms and information content}
\label{subsec:arms}
The LLM and the classical comparators do not necessarily use the same information, and this matters for the interpretation of any cost difference, because ERP safety stock and safety lead time embed accumulated planner judgement, and safety stock and safety lead time are alternative ways of buffering unreliability \cite{vanKampen2010safety}. Planned lead times can also drift upward when they are adjusted dynamically (the ``lead time syndrome'' \cite{thurer2022planned}), which would inflate any comparator that adds them to the lead time. We therefore construct arms that make the information explicit (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

\subsection{Simulation, capacity replay and metrics}
Each pair is simulated independently on the post-cutoff horizon as a single-item, single-echelon continuous-review $(r,Q)$ system \cite{hadley1963analysis, axsater2015inventory, zipkin2000foundations, silver2017inventory, porteus2002foundations} with lost sales \cite{bijvank2011lost}, in the spirit of simulation-based experiments on supply and timing uncertainty \cite{grasso1984simulation}, a shared opening stock, an empty pipeline and arrivals after the rounded mean lead time in working days (Appendix~\ref{app:math}). MOQs are treated as hard order-size constraints, as imposed by suppliers \cite{heese2025moq}, and storage limits as capacity bounds on the replenishment decision \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. The primary metrics are the mean material-horizon holding-plus-ordering cost, the mean material-level fill rate, the aggregate (volume-weighted) fill rate and the number of stockout days. We report paired differences with 10{,}000-sample bootstrap 95\% intervals.

A static check $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$ does not show that storage limits are respected as the inventory evolves, because the reorder point includes lead-time demand and inventory on order can exceed the limit when it arrives. We therefore record, for every policy and every simulated day, the post-receipt on-hand stock and report the share of pairs and days above the limit (the \emph{unconstrained} simulation). We then \emph{replay} every policy with each order truncated to the storage limit minus the current inventory position (the \emph{hard-limit replay}). The hard-limit replay is our primary evaluation.

\subsection{Repeated runs and sensitivities}
To examine run-to-run variation, the entire cohort is generated three times (runs 1, 2 and 3) with the same prompt file and the same data. Run~1 serves as the reference for tabulated results unless stated otherwise; runs 2 and 3 provide replicates. Sensitivity analyses vary the working-day reconstruction (majority, any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals), the shortage penalty (zero, one and five times unit cost), and the exclusion of diagnostic outliers (52 pairs with coefficient-of-variation error above 0.75 or safety stock outside half to twice the reference).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Completion and policy characteristics}
All four runs (three with the full input and one ablation, Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) return a valid, storage-feasible $(r,Q)$ policy for all 365 pairs after at most three attempts per pair. Retries were mostly caused by schema errors (invalid policy), not by capacity violations. The validity gate rejects non-positive reorder points and order quantities, negative safety stocks and reorder points below the safety stock. The ERP safety stock is zero for 198 of the 365 pairs (188 of the 346 in the cohort below), so for a majority of pairs the SAP-derived arm holds no safety stock at all.

\subsection{Headline comparison with ERP-derived parameters}
Unless stated otherwise, all results from here on use the hard-limit replay (every order truncated at the storage limit) on the 346-pair common-feasible cohort, with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty; Section~\ref{subsec:capacity} reports the unconstrained simulation for comparison. Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results} compares all arms. In run~1 the LLM policies cost 11.4\% less than SAP-derived policies and 30.5\% less than the SLT-informed OR comparator (mean 2{,}928 versus 3{,}305 and 4{,}211). The mean fill rate is 0.18 points lower than for SAP-derived (98.11\% versus 98.30\%; bootstrap interval $-0.54$ to $+0.10$) and 0.11 points lower than for the SLT-informed OR. The mean cost difference against SAP-derived is $-377$ (95\% interval $-651$ to $-154$); the LLM is cheaper than SAP-derived in 241 of 346 pairs and, in 226 pairs, simultaneously no more expensive and no worse in fill. The LLM incurs more stockout days in total (910 versus 804 and 831), and aggregate fill is 78.87\% versus 79.34\% and 79.15\%. Aggregate fill is dominated by a few high-demand materials: in the unconstrained simulation of run~1, excluding the five largest materials raises all three to 94.6--95.8\% (Figure~\ref{fig:publication-summary-graphics}).

The cost decomposition shows where the saving comes from. Relative to SAP-derived parameters the LLM carries about 17\% less average inventory (16{,}256 versus 19{,}565 units) and has about 16\% lower holding cost, while it places about the same number of orders per year (17.3 versus 17.6). The saving is therefore a trade of inventory for service: the LLM holds less stock and accepts about 13\% more stockout days. It is not the consequence of a better ordering rhythm, and it is not free of service cost. Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that, once shortages carry a cost, this trade no longer favours the LLM.

\subsection{Information asymmetry: what the LLM adds beyond the ERP inputs}
\label{subsec:arms-results}
Table~\ref{tab:arms-results} compares the arms in the hard-limit replay on the 346 pairs of the cohort, for all three runs. Three results stand out.

First, the SLT-informed OR and its ERP-floor variant are the most expensive arms (4{,}211 and 4{,}248). The ERP safety lead time inflates their effective lead time, hence their buffers; against them the LLM is about 30--31\% cheaper in every run. This comparison therefore mainly measures how heavily ERP inputs over-buffer, not the quality of the LLM.

Second, the \emph{history-only OR}, which uses no ERP inputs, has the lowest cost of all non-LLM arms (2{,}764 at a fill of 98.14\%). Against it the LLM is 5.9--6.0\% more expensive in all three runs (run 1: mean difference $+164$, 95\% interval $+91$ to $+240$), with fill differences of $-0.03$, $-0.25$ and $+0.01$ points whose intervals include zero in all three runs. The LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in only 74--77 of 346 pairs. Part of the LLM's lower cost relative to ERP therefore reflects the weakness of the ERP benchmarks and not LLM superiority.

Third, the LLM is nonetheless cheaper than ERP-derived parameters (11.3--11.4\%) at a mean fill that is lower by 0.14--0.40 points (the interval excludes zero only in run~2), so it is useful when the alternative is the status quo, and it approaches a competent OR benchmark without code or training. Whether the LLM relies on the ERP planning parameters it is given is tested in the ablation of Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}.

\subsection{LLM without ERP planning parameters (ablation)}
\label{subsec:ablation}
In this ablation the LLM receives exactly the same material histories, cost parameters, MOQ, service target and storage limit as in runs 1--3, but the ERP safety stock and ERP safety lead time are blanked in the uploaded CSV and marked ``not supplied'' in the inline parameter block; the system prompt file is unchanged. It tests whether the LLM anchors on the ERP planning parameters it is given, and therefore whether the comparison with the ERP-informed arms is confounded by information. The ablation run has the same structure as runs 1--3: all 365 pairs returned a valid, storage-compliant policy.

\emph{Policy values.} Withholding the ERP parameters changes the LLM's policies only slightly beyond its ordinary run-to-run variation. The median ratios of safety stock and reorder point to run~1 are both 1.00 (346 common pairs). The reorder point differs by more than 10\% in 54.6\% of pairs, against 48.3\% between runs~1 and~3 and 48.0\% between runs~2 and~3 with the ERP parameters supplied; the safety stock differs by more than 10\% in 11.0\% of pairs, against 9.8\% and 9.5\%. The median absolute reorder-point difference is 128 units, against 90 and 84 between ERP-supplied runs. The mean ratio is slightly above one (1.04 for safety stock, 1.05 for the reorder point), so the LLM tends to buffer a little more when it has no ERP anchor.

\emph{Outcomes.} In the hard-limit replay the LLM without ERP parameters has a mean cost of 3{,}014 at a mean fill of 98.06\%, against 2{,}928--2{,}931 and 97.89--98.16\% in runs~1--3. It remains 8.8\% cheaper than SAP-derived parameters (95\% interval of the mean difference $-566$ to $-61$; fill $-0.24$ points, interval including zero) and 28.4--29.1\% cheaper than the SLT-informed and ERP-floor ORs, but it is 9.0\% more expensive than the history-only OR (interval $+134$ to $+387$; fill $-0.09$ points; cheaper in 91 of 346 pairs). In the unconstrained simulation the corresponding figures are $-7.0\%$ against SAP-derived (interval $-513$ to $+16$, which includes zero), $-23.2\%$ and $-23.8\%$ against the two ERP-informed ORs, and $+8.8\%$ against the history-only OR. Withholding the ERP parameters thus raises the LLM's cost by roughly 3 percentage points relative to the history-only OR and does not reverse any ranking.

\emph{By plant.} The pattern is the same as with ERP inputs: the LLM remains cheaper than SAP-derived at Plants A (215 pairs, $-10.8\%$) and B (65 pairs, $-21.9\%$) and more expensive at Plant C (66 pairs, $+41.6\%$ against $+36.2\%$ in run~1; cheaper in 8 pairs). The Plant~C loss is therefore not caused by the LLM leaning on ERP parameters either.

\emph{Interpretation.} Two conclusions follow. First, the information-asymmetry concern (that the LLM benefits from ERP inputs) does not explain the LLM's performance: with the ERP parameters removed the LLM keeps most of its advantage over ERP-derived parameters, and the gap to the history-only OR widens only moderately. Second, the ERP parameters are of limited use to the LLM; most of its output is determined by the demand and lead-time histories. A practical consequence is that the LLM can be deployed for materials whose ERP planning data are missing or stale. One limitation is that the ablation tests only the two ERP planning fields; the LLM still sees unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target and storage limit, which all reference arms also use.

\subsection{Storage limits along simulated trajectories}
\label{subsec:capacity}
All 365 LLM policies satisfy the static rule $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$. Along the simulated trajectories, however, the unconstrained simulation exceeds the limit on post-receipt on-hand stock for 183 pairs (52.9\%) for the LLM (10.2\% of simulated pair-days; median peak 1.02 times the limit; maximum 2.52), 198 pairs (57.2\%; 15.9\% of days) for SAP-derived and 230 pairs (66.5\%; 23.4\% of days) for the SLT-informed OR; no pair starts above the limit. The three LLM runs give 183, 181 and 178 pairs. Exceedances arise because inventory on order and lead-time demand can add to stock above $SS+Q$; the static check is therefore necessary but not sufficient, and it applies to every method, not just the LLM.

Under the hard-limit replay no arm exceeds the limit by more than one unit (rounding). Enforcing the limit raises the LLM mean cost from 2{,}850 to 2{,}928 and lowers its mean fill by 0.04 points (98.15\% to 98.11\%); SAP-derived rises from 3{,}184 to 3{,}305 and the SLT-informed OR from 3{,}852 to 4{,}211, with the latter placing 24.5 instead of 15.8 orders on average. The ranking among arms is unchanged, and the LLM's relative advantage over SAP-derived is 11.4\% instead of 10.5\%. We therefore use the replay as the primary evaluation.

\subsection{Plants and demand regimes}
\label{subsec:plants}
The average hides a strong plant effect (Table~\ref{tab:plants}, hard-limit replay, run~1). The LLM is 14.1\% cheaper than SAP-derived in Plant A (cheaper in 179 of 215 pairs) and 22.7\% cheaper in Plant B (54 of 65), but 36.2\% \emph{more} expensive in Plant C, where it is cheaper in only 8 of 66 pairs. The Plant~C loss is similar in the other full runs ($+32.2\%$ and $+31.5\%$ in runs~2 and~3) and larger without the ERP parameters ($+41.6\%$). Plant C is also the plant whose calendar we repaired; Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that the loss persists under every working-day reconstruction in which Plant C can be scored. Against the history-only OR the LLM is 7.2\% costlier in Plant A, 3.6\% in Plant B and 6.7\% in Plant C (run~1), so no plant favours the LLM over a plain history-based $(r,Q)$ policy. Note that the SLT-informed and history-only ORs give identical results in Plant C, because the ERP safety lead time is zero for all 66 Plant~C pairs.

By demand regime, the 33 lumpy materials show the largest cost reduction against SAP-derived ($-25.4\%$ in run~1; $-23.7\%$ to $-25.2\%$ in the other runs), whereas the 313 smooth materials show $-9.8\%$ at a 0.24-point lower mean fill (98.64\% versus 98.88\%). The service effect for lumpy materials is not consistent (mean fill $+0.38$ points in run~1, $-0.29$ in run~2), and the history-only OR is again 9\% cheaper than the LLM there while achieving a much lower aggregate fill (54.3\% versus 57.0\% for the LLM and 65.8\% for SAP-derived), so part of any cost saving on lumpy materials is bought with service. With only 33 lumpy materials we regard this as indicative only. By demand variability (terciles of the coefficient of variation), the LLM is 15.7\% cheaper than SAP-derived at equal fill in the lowest tercile, 7.5\% cheaper with a 0.68-point lower mean fill in the middle tercile (98.10\% versus 98.78\%), and 12.9\% cheaper in the highest tercile.

\subsection{Run-to-run stability}
\label{subsec:stability}
Across the three runs the aggregate results are very similar: LLM cost relative to SAP-derived is $-10.5\%$, $-9.8\%$ and $-10.2\%$ in the unconstrained simulation, and $-11.4\%$, $-11.3\%$ and $-11.4\%$ in the hard-limit replay, so the spread is below one percentage point. The distributions of policy values are near-identical (median reorder point 1{,}584, 1{,}582 and 1{,}466; median order quantity 2{,}737, 2{,}777 and 2{,}693; median safety stock 683, 688 and 669; Figure~\ref{fig:three-run-reproducibility}). Individual policies, however, vary. Comparing runs 2 and 3 on the 365 pairs, all three policy values are identical for only 36 pairs; every policy resolves to the $(r,Q)$ family, and the median absolute differences are 81 units for the reorder point, 6 for the order quantity and 3 for the safety stock. A few pairs vary strongly (for one material the reorder point was 14{,}882, 14{,}947 and 29{,}218 units in the three runs; for two others the safety stock was zero in one run and 660 or 1{,}075 units in the others). The practical implication is that one generation per material is a noisy estimate whose aggregate behaviour is stable but whose individual values should be reviewed. Non-determinism of this kind is a documented property of LLMs \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}, so that repeated-run reporting should be standard.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}
\paragraph{Plant C calendar.} Plant C's source working-day flag is all zero, which required reconstruction. We re-scored all policies (hard-limit replay) under four alternatives: any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals. In every variant in which Plant C can be scored (any-plant and calendar-day arrivals), the LLM remains 31--35\% more expensive than SAP-derived in Plant C across the three full runs. The cohort-wide advantage over SAP-derived is $-10.8\%$ to $-11.1\%$ in these variants (primary calendar: $-11.3\%$ to $-11.4\%$), and the cost excess over the history-only OR is 5.6--6.1\%. The strict and exclusion variants (280 pairs) drop Plant C and show a larger LLM advantage over SAP-derived ($-17.0\%$ to $-17.6\%$) and a 5.8--6.6\% excess over the history-only OR. The Plant C result is therefore not an artefact of our calendar repair. One residual risk remains: the LLM saw demand series for Plant C that were themselves built on the repaired calendar, and only a re-generation with alternative inputs could remove that dependence.

\paragraph{Shortage penalty.} With a positive lost-sales penalty the zero-penalty cost ranking changes. Table~\ref{tab:penalty} gives mean total cost per pair (holding, ordering and shortage cost) in the hard-limit replay for penalties of zero, one and five times the unit cost. The SAP-derived parameters become the cheapest arm as soon as shortages are priced, because they carry more inventory and fewer stockouts. Run~1 of the LLM then costs 2.4\% more than SAP-derived at both one and five times the unit cost; it remains cheaper than the SLT-informed OR (1.2\% lower at both multiples) and the ERP-floor OR, and slightly cheaper than the history-only OR (0.8\% lower at both multiples). The LLM's cost advantage over ERP-derived parameters is therefore specific to the case in which service shortfalls are not charged, and the service deficit is a real cost when shortages are priced.

\section{Managerial Insights}
\label{sec:managerial}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Position the tool as a reviewed decision aid, not an autonomous policy engine.} Single generations differ at the material level, and roughly 6--8\% of outputs carry a free-text escalation flag. The gate-simulate-review structure (Section~\ref{subsec:rules}) captures most of the value of the LLM (a candidate policy for every material, with a justification) while leaving the decision to the planner.
\item \textbf{Benchmark against a history-only OR policy before claiming value.} ERP-derived parameters and ERP-informed OR policies are weak benchmarks: safety stocks are often stale or zero and safety lead times inflate the effective lead time. A planning team that evaluates an LLM aid only against ERP will overestimate its benefit. The history-only $(r,Q)$ is cheap to compute and was cheaper than the LLM in our data.
\item \textbf{Where the LLM is useful.} Plants A and B (cost reductions of 14--23\% against ERP), lumpy materials (about 25\% cheaper than ERP-derived parameters, although with mixed service effects and only 33 materials) and the many materials with no analyst time are the sweet spot. Plant C, where the LLM loses, indicates that conditions with repaired calendars, small samples or atypical lead-time behaviour need escalation.
\item \textbf{Verify storage along the trajectory.} The static rule $SS+Q\le\mathrm{limit}$ cannot protect a warehouse: 53--66\% of pairs breach the limit in simulation under every method. Operations should enforce a cap in the order-release logic (order-up-to or truncation), or treat the limit as a design requirement in the policy.
\item \textbf{Price shortages explicitly.} Without a shortage penalty the cost ranking rewards under-stocking. With a penalty of one unit cost the LLM's cost advantage over SAP disappears (it is about 2.4\% more expensive). Planners should set service targets and penalties before reading the cost comparison.
\item \textbf{Freeze and log the configuration.} Treat the system prompt as a controlled file, log every call (input hash, time, session, raw response) and re-run before changing the prompt. Our reproducibility argument rests on this log, not on the platform's model identifier, which the client cannot see.
\item \textbf{Cost and latency.} Each LLM generation took about 7--20 minutes, whereas the OR baseline takes milliseconds. The LLM is attractive where analyst time and not compute is scarce, but a deployment needs batching and monitoring. Periodic regeneration as demand and lead times change would also support adaptable planning under uncertainty \cite{sengupta2025uncertainty}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Research Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
In a three-plant industrial dataset, a prompt-configured LLM generates feasible, reviewable $(r,Q)$ policies for every material. They are cheaper than ERP-derived parameters in the simulation, but they do not beat a history-only OR policy. We therefore describe InventOR as a way to make a competent analyst's workflow available at scale, not as a method that outperforms optimization. The information-controlled evaluation is what allows this conclusion; without the ERP-independent arm, the first-cut result ($-11.4\%$ against SAP and $-30.5\%$ against the SLT-informed OR in the hard-limit replay) would have been presented as superiority.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) The data come from one manufacturer and three plants; generalization to other settings is not shown. (ii) The platform does not expose the model identifier or inference settings, so the model is operator-declared. (iii) The simulation excludes observed receipts, corrections and scrap and starts with an empty pipeline; a prospective field trial with planners is needed to assess realized benefit, which we do not claim. (iv) The cost model has zero shortage penalty in the primary results and standard holding and ordering costs; with a positive penalty the cost ranking changes. (v) The $(r,Q)$ policy is a single-item, single-echelon family; multi-item coupling and shared capacity are outside the scope. (vi) Run-to-run variation of individual policies is substantial. (vii) Plant C required calendar reconstruction. (viii) No planner outcomes were measured: we make no claim about planner workload or decision quality. (ix) The reference arms are normal-approximation $(r,Q)$ policies; we did not include an intermittent-demand-specific baseline (for example Croston- or SBA-based forecasts \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy}) or a trained data-driven or reinforcement-learning policy, so the comparison with the state of the art is incomplete, particularly for lumpy materials.

\paragraph{Research perspectives.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Ensembling and consistency.} Aggregating several generations (median or consensus policies) may reduce the individual-policy noise while retaining the LLM's justification; the copilot findings for spare parts \cite{nakhost2025copilot} suggest this is promising.
\item \emph{Hybrid generators.} Combining the history-only OR with the LLM (the LLM reviewing and annotating the OR policy, or choosing among candidates by regime), as proposed for LLM--OR integration \cite{wasserkrug2025enhancing}, could capture the OR's cost level and the LLM's flags.
\item \emph{Comparison with trained methods.} A benchmark that compares prompt-based generators with trained data-driven or reinforcement-learning policies \cite{gabellini2026novel, kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl} on identical data and information would clarify the trade-off between setup effort and policy quality.
\item \emph{Intermittent-demand and richer policy families.} Allowing order-up-to or $(s,S)$ policies and regime-specific estimators may help the lumpy and Plant C pairs \cite{prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}.
\item \emph{Planner-in-the-loop field trials.} Prospective studies should measure planner time, override rates and realized service and inventory, which our simulation cannot, and should examine adoption drivers and barriers with mixed-methods designs \cite{venkatesh2023adoption, cannas2023aiscm, trifone2026agentic}.
\item \emph{Standards for information-controlled evaluation.} Studies of LLM decision aids should report an ERP-independent baseline, an ablation that withholds the incumbent's inputs, trajectory-level feasibility, and run-to-run variation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
InventOR shows that a prompt-configured LLM can generate feasible, justified inventory policies for every material of an industrial portfolio without training, and that a deterministic gate and simulation make these proposals reviewable. On a three-plant dataset, the proposals cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at slightly lower service, but about 6\% more than a history-only OR policy; when lost sales are priced at one unit cost or more, ERP-derived parameters become the cheapest arm. Storage limits that hold in a static check are exceeded along simulated trajectories for every method, and the LLM loses to ERP at one plant. We therefore recommend the LLM as a human-reviewed aid benchmarked against history-only OR, with trajectory-level storage checks, explicit shortage pricing and a frozen, logged configuration. These results are simulated-comparison evidence, not field evidence of improved planning outcomes.

\section{Formal Definitions}
\label{app:math}

\paragraph{Reference calculation.}
These are the standard textbook calculations \cite{silver2017inventory, axsater2015inventory}. For pair $i$, let $\bar d_i$ and $\sigma_{D,i}$ be the mean and standard deviation of pre-cutoff working-day demand, $L_i$ and $\sigma_{L,i}$ the mean and standard deviation of the lead time in working days, $K_i$ the ordering cost, $h_i$ the annual holding cost per unit (unit cost times holding rate), $z_i$ the standard normal quantile of the service target, and $250$ the working days per year. The order quantity is $Q_i=\max\{\mathrm{MOQ}_i,\lceil\sqrt{2K_i\cdot250\bar d_i/h_i}\,\rceil\}$. The lead-time-demand standard deviation is $\sigma_{LTD,i}=\sqrt{L_i\sigma_{D,i}^2+\bar d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2}$, the safety stock $SS_i=\lceil z_i\sigma_{LTD,i}\rceil$ and the reorder point $R_i=\lceil\bar d_iL_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+SS_i\rceil$, where $L^{\mathrm{eff}}_i$ is the mean lead time for the history-only OR and the mean lead time plus the ERP safety lead time for the SLT-informed OR and the ERP-floor OR. The SAP-derived arm uses the ERP safety stock and the same $Q_i$. All policies are projected to $SS_i+Q_i\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ when $\mathrm{MOQ}_i+SS_i$ fits.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
On each post-cutoff working day, demand is served from on-hand stock, unmet demand is lost, and an order of size $Q_i$ is placed when the inventory position (on-hand plus on order) is at or below $R_i$ and arrives after the rounded mean lead time in subsequent working observations. Observed receipts, corrections and scrap are ignored. The total cost is $TC_i=h_i\bar I_iT/250+K_iN_i$, where $\bar I_i$ is the mean on-hand stock over the $T$ simulated working days and $N_i$ the number of orders. Fill rate is served divided by demanded units. In the hard-limit replay an order is truncated to $\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ minus the inventory position, and no order is placed if the truncated quantity is zero.

\section{Reproducibility Record}
\label{app:repro}
Prompt file, runner code, evaluation scripts, aggregate outputs and per-call input hashes are distributed with the code release (Data and Code Availability). The three runs and the ablation use the same prompt file; the ablation differs only in that the two ERP planning fields are blanked in the uploaded data and marked ``not supplied'' in the inline block. The deployed system prompt is the file \texttt{system\_prompt.txt} in the release. The platform does not expose the model identifier or inference settings to the client; the model is therefore operator-declared (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 on an enterprise platform with a code-interpreter tool) and not independently verified.
