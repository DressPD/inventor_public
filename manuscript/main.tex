\begin{abstract}
Material planners must set a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity for thousands of plant-material pairs, usually under ERP parameters that were last revised long ago. We define and evaluate \emph{InventOR}, a decision support system (DSS) for this recurring decision. For each material, a large language model (LLM) configured only through a prompt, without training or fine-tuning, proposes a reorder-point/order-quantity $(r,Q)$ policy with a written justification and an escalation flag. Deterministic software gates the proposal against minimum order quantities and storage limits and replays it on held-out demand before the planner, who remains the decision-maker, reviews it. We derive six design requirements and evaluate the artifact on 365 plant-material pairs from three plants of an industrial manufacturer, using three independent LLM runs, seven reference arms and an ablation that withholds the ERP planning parameters. In a hard-limit storage replay, the LLM policies cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at a mean fill rate 0.1--0.4 points lower, but 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile $(r,Q)$ baselines. The saving comes from carrying 13--17\% less inventory with 11--20\% more stockout days; when shortages are priced at one unit cost, ERP-derived parameters are cheaper and a simulation-tuned policy is cheapest. Static storage checks are insufficient: simulated trajectories exceed storage limits for 53--66\% of pairs. InventOR is a checkable, human-reviewed candidate generator, not an optimizer that beats data-driven baselines; benefits to planners are untested. We contribute design principles, review rules and a research agenda.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Replenishment parameters in manufacturing are set per plant-material pair: a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity. In large ERP systems these values often remain at levels set years earlier, while demand, supplier performance and storage constraints change. Revisiting them requires analysts who can fit demand and lead-time statistics, apply inventory theory, respect minimum order quantities (MOQs) and storage limits, and explain the result to planners. A conversational large language model (LLM) with a code interpreter appears to offer this scarce skill, and a growing literature explores such tools for supply-chain and operations decisions \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, trifone2026agentic}.

Three problems limit its use. First, LLM outputs can be unfaithful or fabricated \cite{ji2023survey}, so a proposed policy is a candidate, not a decision. Second, superiority claims over ERP parameters are hard to interpret when the LLM and the comparators receive different information. Third, a storage rule that holds when a policy is written down can be violated as stock builds up over time.

This paper treats the problem as one of decision support system (DSS) design. We define \emph{InventOR} as a human-reviewed, model-driven DSS for the recurring decision of setting replenishment parameters per plant-material pair (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). A planner remains the decision-maker. The system supplies, for each material, a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy proposed by an enterprise LLM application that is configured only through a prompt and receives no project-specific training or fine-tuning, together with a justification, an escalation flag and a counterfactual simulation; deterministic software parses, gates and simulates every proposal before the planner reviews it and alone decides whether any ERP parameter is changed.

We follow a design-science logic \cite{hevner2004design, peffers2007design}: we state problem and scope, derive design requirements, build the artifact and evaluate it on real industrial data. Four research questions structure the paper: how an LLM should be embedded so that its proposals are bounded, verifiable, reproducible and reviewable (RQ1); what cost and service they achieve when the information available to each approach is controlled (RQ2); whether they respect storage limits along simulated trajectories and are stable across runs (RQ3); and when a planner should trust, escalate or reject a proposal (RQ4).

Our contributions are: (C1) a definition and scope of the DSS, with six design requirements that make the role of the LLM explicit and bounded (Section~\ref{sec:scope}); (C2) an architecture and operating rules that separate generation, deterministic gating, simulation and planner review, with trust, escalate and reject rules and design principles drawn from the application (Sections~\ref{sec:aid} and \ref{sec:discussion}); (C3) an information-controlled, trajectory-level evaluation with seven reference arms, an LLM ablation denied the ERP planning parameters and a hard-limit storage replay as the primary result (Section~\ref{sec:design}); and (C4) complete-cohort industrial evidence from three LLM runs on 365 plant-material pairs, with managerial and research implications.

Our main findings are as follows. Against ERP-derived parameters the LLM policies are about 11\% cheaper at a mean fill rate 0.1--0.4 points lower, but the LLM costs 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile $(r,Q)$ baselines at essentially equal service, and its saving over ERP comes from carrying less inventory at the price of more stockout days. Storage limits that hold in a static check are violated along unconstrained trajectories for 53--66\% of pairs. The simulation therefore does not show superior optimization by the LLM; it shows that a prompt-configured LLM can produce checkable candidate policies without code or training, whose benefit to planners remains to be tested.


\section{Problem, Scope and Definition}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{The decision problem}
\label{subsec:decision}
The decision supported is the periodic (re)setting of a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity for each plant-material pair held in an ERP system. In the classification of Gorry and Scott Morton \cite{gorry1971framework} it is a partly structured operational-control decision: the calculation is textbook, but the choice of estimator, the treatment of intermittent or trending demand, the plausibility of recorded lead times and the handling of constraints require judgement that is applied inconsistently when thousands of materials compete for a few analysts' time. The decision-maker is the material planner; hard constraints are the minimum order quantity (MOQ) and a physical storage limit; and feedback is slow, because a changed parameter shows its effect only in later demand. The scope is one pair at a time (single item, single echelon, continuous review, lost sales, $(r,Q)$ family); order release, supplier selection, forecasting-model development and multi-echelon networks are out of scope, and the LLM is a candidate generator that does not forecast, place orders, simulate or decide.

\subsection{Definition and level of automation}
\label{subsec:definition}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Definition.} \emph{InventOR is a human-reviewed, model-driven decision support system that, for one plant-material pair at a time, (i) obtains from a large language model, configured only by a written prompt, a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; (ii) verifies the candidate deterministically against explicit constraints; (iii) evaluates it by counterfactual simulation against reference policies on held-out demand; and (iv) presents the result to a planner, who alone decides whether ERP parameters are changed.}
\end{quote}
In the classical DSS architecture of data, model and dialog components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, the LLM and the simulator form the model component, the material-scoped data extract and the prompt form the data and knowledge components, and the review interface is the dialog component (Section~\ref{subsec:architecture}). In terms of the levels of automation of Parasuraman et al. \cite{parasuraman2000model}, InventOR operates at a low-to-intermediate level: it selects and suggests one alternative and leaves execution to the human. Accepting automated suggestions invites complacency and automation bias \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}, which the review rules of Section~\ref{subsec:rules} are meant to counter.

\subsection{Design requirements}
\label{subsec:requirements}
The requirements follow from the known unreliability of LLM outputs \cite{ji2023survey}, guidance on human--AI interaction and accountability \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, run-to-run nondeterminism of LLM inference \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism} and the constraints of inventory control. Table~\ref{tab:requirements} ties each to a design feature (Section~\ref{sec:aid}) and an evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Decision support systems and human--AI decision making.}
DSS research has long distinguished data-, model- and knowledge-driven systems and has stressed that the value of a DSS lies in improving the judgement of a human decision-maker rather than in replacing it \cite{gorry1971framework, sprague1980framework, shim2002past}. Research on human--automation interaction shows that the way suggestions are scoped, presented and verified determines whether users rely on them appropriately or over-rely on them \cite{parasuraman2000model, parasuraman2010complacency},. Recent DSS work derives design principles for LLM-based assistants \cite{schelhorn2026design}, reports design and pilot implementations of AI-enabled decision support in operations \cite{prithvirajan2026aidss}, and shows that the uptake of algorithmic advice depends on transparency and on the user's metacognition \cite{ning2024transparency, soleimanof2026metacognition}; evaluations of data-driven forecasting systems in practice likewise stress lessons beyond accuracy \cite{chae2024restaurant}. Model-driven support also shapes how groups handle disagreement \cite{engin2024conflict}, and human override of algorithmic safety-stock advice is a documented risk \cite{wang2024aversion}; InventOR therefore keeps the planner as decision-maker and makes the evidence for each proposal explicit.

\paragraph{LLMs in operations and supply-chain decisions.}
Reviews and frameworks map the opportunities for generative AI in supply chains \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, li2024genaiscm, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, boone2025generative} and stress that adoption depends on assigned human roles, data quality and governance \cite{trifone2026agentic, cannas2023aiscm, venkatesh2023adoption, cohen2026supply, olan2025xai}. Evidence from manufacturing planning is thin: a fine-tuned LLM for digital-manufacturing optimization \cite{amarasinghe2026finetuned}, a transformer trained to set order timing and quantity and benchmarked against $(r,Q)$ \cite{liu2026transformer}, and experiments showing that ChatGPT reproduces human decision biases of managers \cite{chen2025manager}. Most technical LLM work translates problem statements into optimization models and solver code \cite{li2023large, huang2025orlm, ahmaditeshnizi2024optimus, zhang2025or}, studies multi-agent control in inventory flows \cite{quan2024invagent, jannelli2025agentic}, disruption modelling \cite{lin2025unisone} or classical OR tasks such as routing \cite{hamdan2026routing}. The closest LLM study to ours uses an LLM as a forecasting copilot for spare-parts demand, combined with simulation, and reports high variability for irregular demand that can be reduced by averaging repeated queries and enriching prompts \cite{nakhost2025copilot}. We test run-to-run variability directly (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}); the difference is that our LLM proposes a complete policy, not a forecast.

\paragraph{Data-driven inventory control without LLMs.}
Learning-based replenishment is well established, including data-driven policies learned from sequentially observed non-stationary demand \cite{ren2024datadriven} and forecasts augmented with leading indicators \cite{sagaert2025leading}, and heuristics remain competitive in constrained settings such as perishable inventory \cite{gulecyuz2025heuristic}. Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to omnichannel and multi-echelon systems \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl, ma2026omnichannel} and aerospace replenishment \cite{wang2022aerospace}; deep-learning forecasting has been integrated with replenishment \cite{ma2026integrated}; contextual distributionally robust optimization uses side information to set order quantities \cite{wang2026contextual}; and distribution-free online algorithms handle capacitated warehouses under uncertain price and demand \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. The closest operational neighbour is a trained data-driven framework that jointly sets replenishment parameters, safety stock and safety lead time on real automotive data \cite{gabellini2026novel}. Reinforcement learning has also been combined with demand forecasting to optimize order points \cite{schett2026rlorder}. These methods need training or tuned hyperparameters; InventOR needs none, at the price of a generator whose logic cannot be inspected beyond its written justification.

\paragraph{Intermittent and spare-parts demand.}
Material histories in manufacturing are frequently intermittent or lumpy. The statistical literature offers specialized estimators and classification schemes \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy, willemain2004new, prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, and recent studies develop feature-based forecast combinations and temporal aggregation for intermittent demand \cite{sanguri2023intermittent, li2022featurebased}, forecasting for spare parts across product life-cycle phases \cite{goldsmith2025spare}, and integrated forecasting and planning or multi-echelon stocking models for repairable and spare parts \cite{babaveisi2022repairable, caserta2026amazon}. Manufacturers' choice of safety buffers is examined in \cite{hedvall2025buffers}.

In summary, InventOR is not a new inventory model and does not claim to beat trained or optimized methods; it contributes a DSS design with a bounded LLM generator, an information-controlled evaluation and industrial evidence. The $(r,Q)$ policy it uses is the standard workhorse of continuous-review control \cite{hadley1963analysis, porteus2002foundations, silver2017inventory}.

\section{The InventOR Decision Support System}
\label{sec:aid}

\subsection{Architecture and components}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Following the classical DSS components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, InventOR has a \emph{data} component (a material-scoped extract of daily demand, lead times, working-day flags, cost and constraint parameters and ERP reference values; the fields supplied are controlled and logged; DR4), a \emph{knowledge} component (the system prompt file defining the task, the prohibited methods, the storage rules and the output schema, Supplementary File S1; DR1, DR5), a \emph{model} component split into a generator (an enterprise LLM application with a code interpreter, configured only by the prompt, proposing one $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; DR1) and a deterministic verifier and simulator (parser, gate, held-out simulation with hard storage-limit replay and reference arms; DR2, DR3), a \emph{dialog} component (policy, justification, flag and simulated comparison shown to the planner under the trust, escalate and reject rules; DR6) and a \emph{governance} layer (frozen prompt file, per-call log of input hash, time, session and raw response, repeated runs; DR5). The generator is the only non-inspectable component. In the evaluated prototype the dialog component is the per-pair output record; no interactive interface was built or evaluated.

\subsection{Operation in four steps}
For one material, InventOR answers how much safety stock to hold, at what level to reorder and how much to order, in four steps (Figure~\ref{fig:tool_process_map}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{Figure_1_Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{InventOR workflow: material data are prepared, an LLM proposes a policy, deterministic software parses, checks and simulates it, and a planner reviews the result.}
  \label{fig:tool_process_map}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Generate.} The LLM, accessed through an enterprise application with a code interpreter, receives one material's history (daily demand, realized lead times, working-day flags) and a short parameter block (unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target, storage limit, and current ERP safety stock and safety lead time for comparison). A written prompt (the only configuration; no weights are changed) \cite{sahoo2024systematic} instructs it to compute demand and lead-time statistics, apply an economic-order-quantity (EOQ) argument and a normal-approximation safety stock, respect the storage limit, and report a structured policy with a short justification and escalation flag.
\item \textbf{Gate.} Deterministic code extracts the policy, rejects malformed or infeasible outputs (missing or non-numeric fields, non-positive reorder point or order quantity, negative safety stock, reorder point below safety stock, safety stock plus order quantity above the storage limit) and retries the generation up to three times. The backtest applies one further admission check, that the order quantity is not below the MOQ.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Each accepted policy is replayed on the material's demand observed \emph{after} the data cutoff, a held-out evaluation in the sense of out-of-sample forecast testing \cite{tashman2000out, hewamalage2023forecast}, together with the comparator policies, producing holding and ordering cost, fill rate and stockout days.
\item \textbf{Review.} A planner sees the policy, the LLM's justification and flag, and the simulated comparison. Only the planner changes ERP parameters.
\end{enumerate}

The LLM is a \emph{candidate generator} whose output is never trusted without the gate; it does not forecast, place orders or run the simulation. The prompt prohibits Monte Carlo simulation, distribution fitting, machine-learning libraries and specialized intermittent-demand estimators, so that the policy rests on transparent statistics.

\subsection{Trust, review and reject rules}
\label{subsec:rules}
We propose three rules for planners, in line with guidelines for human--AI interaction and AI auditing \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}. The rules and the 0.5-point threshold are design proposals not evaluated with planners; the trust rule would be met by a minority of pairs (the LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in 74--77 of 346).
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[Trust (apply after review)] when the proposal passes the gate, the simulated trajectory respects the storage limit when orders are truncated at it, and the simulated cost is not above that of the best history-only OR reference (Croston/SBA or empirical quantile) with a fill rate not more than about 0.5 points lower.
\item[Escalate] when the LLM raises an escalation flag, the material is lumpy or has strongly seasonal or trending demand, the plant or calendar data were repaired, or the policy differs strongly between repeated generations.
\item[Reject] when the proposal fails the gate after the retry budget, exceeds the storage limit in the hard-limit replay, or is dominated (higher cost and lower fill) by a history-only reference.
\end{description}

\subsection{Provenance and reproducibility of the configuration}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The system prompt, a plain-text file deployed unchanged in the enterprise application, defines the task, forbidden methods, capacity rules and output schema (Supplementary File S1). Every call logs the SHA-256 of its input, UTC time, session identifier and raw response. The platform does not expose the model identifier or sampling settings; the evaluated model (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 with a code interpreter) is operator-declared. Reproducibility rests on the prompt file, the call logs and three independent runs (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}).

\section{Application and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Evaluation approach}
\label{subsec:evalapproach}
The evaluation addresses the design requirements (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}): validity and feasibility (DR1, DR2), cost and service against reference arms on held-out demand (DR3), the role of the information given to the LLM (DR4), storage along trajectories (DR2), stability across runs (DR5) and escalation (DR6). The primary basis is the hard-limit replay with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty; sensitivity analyses vary these choices. Following design science \cite{hevner2004design, peffers2007design}, the evaluation is artificial and ex post in the framework of Venable et al. \cite{venable2016feds}: real data and a faithful simulation but no live users. It establishes technical efficacy, feasibility and reliability, not usability, adoption or improved planner decisions.

\subsection{Industrial application and data}\label{subsec:data}
The application is a pilot at a large manufacturer. The dataset contains 233{,}078 daily records for 411 plant-material pairs from three plants (A, B and C), with a data cutoff on 10 March 2026 and a post-cutoff horizon observed to 27 August 2026. Of the 411 pairs, 365 are eligible for scoring (234 in Plant A, 65 in Plant B, 66 in Plant C) and are sent to the LLM. For 19 pairs (all in Plant A) the MOQ plus the reference safety stock exceeds the storage limit, so no comparator can satisfy both rules; these are excluded from outcome comparison, leaving a \emph{common-feasible cohort} of 346 pairs (215, 65 and 66). Demand is concentrated (the five largest materials account for 40.3\% of demand). The source working-day flag of Plant C is all zero; we reconstruct it from the same-date majority of Plants A and B and test this repair in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}. Data quality is a known threat to decision support \cite{watts2009data} and inventory records \cite{dehoratius2008inventory, kang2005information, mersereau2013information}.

Each pair is sent as a material-scoped CSV with daily demand, recorded lead times, a working-day flag, the minimum order quantity, unit cost, holding-cost rate, ordering cost, service-level target, the storage limit, and two \emph{ERP planning parameters} (current safety stock and safety lead time), which are withheld in the ablation arm. The CSV also carries supplier and lane context fields that the prompt does not use.

\subsection{Reference arms and information content}
\label{subsec:arms}

The LLM and the classical comparators therefore do not necessarily use the same information, and we construct arms that make it explicit (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

Three further arms use no ERP input and represent stronger data-driven practice. The \emph{Croston/SBA OR} replaces the mean daily demand by the Croston estimate with the Syntetos--Boylan bias correction \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy} (smoothing constant 0.1), uses the root mean squared one-step forecast error as demand spread and the history-only lead-time moments, which suits intermittent demand \cite{willemain2004new}. The \emph{empirical-quantile OR} sets the reorder point to the service-level quantile of historical rolling lead-time demand over the rounded mean lead time. The \emph{simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$} policy selects, from a grid of reserve factors and order-quantity multiples around the history-only policy, the cheapest candidate whose fill rate on the last 250 working days \emph{before the cutoff} meets the service target (or the best-fill candidate if none does). No post-cutoff data are used in any of these arms. We do not implement a deep reinforcement-learning baseline \cite{stranieri2024drl, kolyaei2025rl}; this is a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Simulation, capacity replay and metrics}
Each pair is simulated independently on the post-cutoff horizon as a single-item, single-echelon continuous-review $(r,Q)$ system \cite{hadley1963analysis, axsater2015inventory, zipkin2000foundations, silver2017inventory, porteus2002foundations} with lost sales \cite{bijvank2011lost}, in the spirit of simulation-based experiments on supply and timing uncertainty \cite{grasso1984simulation}, a shared opening stock, an empty pipeline and arrivals after the rounded mean lead time in working days (Appendix~\ref{app:math}). MOQs are treated as hard order-size constraints, as imposed by suppliers \cite{heese2025moq}, and storage limits as capacity bounds on the replenishment decision \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. The primary metrics are the mean material-horizon holding-plus-ordering cost, the mean material-level fill rate, the aggregate (volume-weighted) fill rate and the number of stockout days. We report paired differences with 10{,}000-sample bootstrap 95\% intervals.

A static check $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$ does not show that storage limits hold as inventory evolves, because the reorder point includes lead-time demand and inventory on order can exceed the limit on arrival. We therefore record the post-receipt on-hand stock for every policy and day and report the share of pairs and days above the limit (the \emph{unconstrained} simulation), and we \emph{replay} every policy with each order truncated to the storage limit minus the current inventory position (the \emph{hard-limit replay}), which is our primary evaluation.

\subsection{Repeated runs and sensitivities}
To examine run-to-run variation, the entire cohort is generated three times (runs 1, 2 and 3) with the same prompt file and the same data. Run~1 serves as the reference for tabulated results unless stated otherwise; runs 2 and 3 provide replicates. Sensitivity analyses vary the working-day reconstruction (majority, any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals), the shortage penalty (zero, one and five times unit cost).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Completion and policy characteristics}\label{subsec:completion}
All four runs (three with the full input and one ablation, Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) return a valid, storage-feasible $(r,Q)$ policy for all 365 pairs after at most three attempts per pair. The validity gate rejects non-positive reorder points and order quantities, negative safety stocks and reorder points below the safety stock. The ERP safety stock is zero for 198 of the 365 pairs (188 of the 346 in the cohort below), so for a majority of pairs the SAP-derived arm holds no safety stock at all.

\subsection{Headline comparison with ERP-derived parameters}\label{subsec:headline}
Unless stated otherwise, all results use the hard-limit replay on the 346-pair common-feasible cohort with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty (Section~\ref{subsec:capacity} gives the unconstrained simulation). Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results} compares all arms; its last columns give the LLM cost relative to each arm in the three runs (positive: the LLM costs more). In run~1 the LLM policies cost 11.4\% less than SAP-derived policies and 30.5\% less than the SLT-informed OR (mean 2{,}928 versus 3{,}305 and 4{,}211), at a mean fill 0.18 points lower than SAP-derived (98.11\% versus 98.30\%; interval $-0.54$ to $+0.10$). The mean cost difference against SAP-derived is $-377$ (95\% interval $-651$ to $-154$); the LLM is cheaper in 241 of 346 pairs and, in 226, simultaneously no more expensive and no worse in fill. It incurs more stockout days (910 versus 804).

Relative to SAP-derived parameters the LLM carries about 17\% less average inventory (16{,}256 versus 19{,}565 units) and about 16\% lower holding cost at about the same number of orders per year (17.3 versus 17.6), and it accepts 11--20\% more stockout days (runs 3 and 2). The saving is therefore a trade of inventory for service, not a better ordering rhythm, and Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that once shortages carry a cost this trade no longer favours the LLM.

\subsection{Information asymmetry: what the LLM adds beyond the ERP inputs}
\label{subsec:arms-results}

First, the SLT-informed OR and its ERP-floor variant are the most expensive arms (4{,}211 and 4{,}248): the ERP safety lead time inflates their effective lead time and buffers. Against them the LLM is 30--31\% cheaper in every run, which mainly measures how heavily ERP inputs over-buffer.

Second, the \emph{history-only OR}, which uses no ERP inputs, costs 2{,}764 at a fill of 98.14\%. The LLM costs 5.9--6.0\% more in all three runs (run~1: $+164$, interval $+91$ to $+240$), with fill differences of $-0.03$, $-0.25$ and $+0.01$ points whose intervals include zero, and is cheaper in only 74--77 of 346 pairs. The stronger data-driven arms widen the gap: the LLM costs 8.2--8.4\% more than the Croston/SBA OR (2{,}705, fill 98.30\%; run~1: $+223$, interval $+97$ to $+362$; cheaper in 91--99 pairs) and 17.7--17.8\% more than the empirical-quantile OR (2{,}489, fill 98.35\%; $+439$, interval $+329$ to $+557$; cheaper in 44--49 pairs; mean fill 0.24 points lower). Part of the LLM's advantage over ERP-derived parameters therefore reflects weak ERP benchmarks.

The simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$ policy sits at a different operating point: an aggregate fill of 84.8\% and the lowest inventory (11{,}914 units) but a mean fill of 96.05\% and 1{,}973 stockout days, because it is tuned on the pre-cutoff year. The LLM costs 10.3--10.4\% less at zero penalty with a 2.1-point higher mean fill, but the interval includes zero ($-2{,}527$ to $+857$); once shortages are priced the tuned policy is by far the cheapest arm (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}).

Third, the LLM improves on ERP-derived parameters (11.3--11.4\% cheaper at 0.14--0.40 points lower fill; the interval excludes zero only in run~2) but does not reach the best classical baselines, which also yield a policy for every material at lower cost.

\subsection{LLM without ERP planning parameters (ablation)}
\label{subsec:ablation}
In this ablation the LLM receives the same inputs as in runs 1--3, but the ERP safety stock and safety lead time are blanked in the CSV and marked ``not supplied''; the system prompt file is unchanged. It tests whether the LLM anchors on the ERP planning parameters. All 365 pairs returned a valid, storage-compliant policy, and the policies change only slightly beyond ordinary run-to-run variation: the median ratios of safety stock and reorder point to run~1 are 1.00; the reorder point differs by more than 10\% in 54.6\% of pairs (48.3\% and 48.0\% between ERP-supplied runs) and the safety stock in 11.0\% (9.8\% and 9.5\%); the mean ratio is 1.04 for safety stock and 1.05 for the reorder point. In the hard-limit replay the cost is 3{,}014 at a mean fill of 98.06\%, against 2{,}928--2{,}931 in runs~1--3. It is 8.8\% below SAP-derived parameters (interval $-566$ to $-61$; fill $-0.24$ points, interval including zero) and 28.4--29.1\% below the SLT-informed and ERP-floor ORs, but 9.0\% above the history-only OR (interval $+134$ to $+387$; cheaper in 91 of 346 pairs), 11.4\% above the Croston/SBA OR, 21.1\% above the empirical-quantile OR and 7.7\% below the simulation-tuned policy. By plant the pattern is unchanged ($-10.8\%$ in Plant A, $-21.9\%$ in Plant B, $+41.6\%$ in Plant C against $+36.2\%$ in run~1), so the Plant~C loss does not stem from reliance on ERP parameters. The information-asymmetry concern therefore does not explain the LLM's advantage over ERP-derived parameters, and the ERP planning fields appear to be of limited use to the LLM. The ablation withholds only these two fields; unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target and storage limit remain available, as they are for all reference arms.





\subsection{Storage limits along simulated trajectories}
\label{subsec:capacity}
All 365 LLM policies satisfy the static rule $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$, yet in the unconstrained simulation post-receipt on-hand stock exceeds the limit for 183 pairs (52.9\%; 178--183 across runs; 10.2\% of pair-days) for the LLM, 198 (57.2\%) for SAP-derived and 230 (66.5\%) for the SLT-informed OR, because inventory on order and lead-time demand add to stock above $SS+Q$. The static check is thus necessary but not sufficient for any method. Under the hard-limit replay no arm exceeds the limit by more than one unit; enforcing it raises the LLM mean cost from 2{,}850 to 2{,}928 and SAP-derived from 3{,}184 to 3{,}305 without changing the ranking.

\subsection{Plants and demand regimes}
\label{subsec:plants}
The average hides a strong plant effect (Table~\ref{tab:plants}, hard-limit replay, run~1). The LLM costs 14.1\% less than SAP-derived in Plant A (cheaper in 179 of 215 pairs) and 22.7\% less in Plant B (54 of 65), but 36.2\% \emph{more} in Plant C (cheaper in 8 of 66); the Plant~C loss is similar in the other full runs ($+32.2\%$, $+31.5\%$) and persists under every working-day reconstruction in which Plant C can be scored (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}). Against the data-driven baselines the LLM costs more in every plant: 7.2\%, 3.6\% and 6.7\% more than the history-only OR, 13.1\%, 2.3\% and 6.2\% more than the Croston/SBA OR, and 20.8\%, 7.7\% and 29.5\% more than the empirical-quantile OR in Plants A, B and C. The SLT-informed and history-only ORs coincide in Plant C because the ERP safety lead time is zero for all its pairs.

By demand regime, the 33 lumpy materials show the largest reduction against SAP-derived ($-25.4\%$ in run~1) and the 313 smooth materials $-9.8\%$ at a 0.24-point lower mean fill. On lumpy materials the service effect is inconsistent (mean fill $+0.38$ points in run~1, $-0.29$ in run~2), the history-only and Croston/SBA ORs cost about 9--10\% less than the LLM, and only the empirical-quantile OR is slightly more expensive (1.2\%); with 33 materials this is indicative only. By terciles of the coefficient of variation, the LLM costs 15.7\%, 7.5\% and 12.9\% less than SAP-derived (lowest to highest), with a 0.68-point lower mean fill in the middle tercile.

\subsection{Run-to-run stability}
\label{subsec:stability}
Across the three runs the aggregate results are very similar: the LLM cost relative to SAP-derived is $-11.4\%$, $-11.3\%$ and $-11.4\%$, and the policy-value medians are close (reorder point 1{,}584, 1{,}582 and 1{,}466; order quantity 2{,}737, 2{,}777 and 2{,}693; safety stock 683, 688 and 669). Individual policies vary, however. Between runs 2 and 3 on the 365 pairs, all three values are identical for only 36 pairs; every policy resolves to the $(r,Q)$ family, and the median absolute differences are 81 units (reorder point), 6 (order quantity) and 3 (safety stock). The reorder point differs by more than 10\% in about 48\% of pairs, and a few pairs vary strongly (one reorder point was 14{,}882, 14{,}947 and 29{,}218 units in the three runs). Aggregate behaviour is reproducible, but one generation per material is a noisy estimate whose individual values should be reviewed, in line with the documented non-determinism of LLMs \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}.


\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}
\paragraph{Plant C calendar.} Plant C's working-day flag required reconstruction, so we re-scored all policies under four alternatives (any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C, calendar-day arrivals). Where Plant C can be scored, the LLM remains 31--35\% more expensive than SAP-derived there in the three full runs, and the cohort-wide advantage is $-10.8\%$ to $-11.1\%$; the strict and exclusion variants (280 pairs) show $-17.0\%$ to $-17.6\%$. The Plant C result is therefore not an artefact of the repair; a residual risk is that the LLM saw Plant C demand series built on the repaired calendar.

\paragraph{Shortage penalty.} Table~\ref{tab:penalty} gives mean total cost per pair (holding, ordering and shortage cost) in the hard-limit replay for penalties of one and five times the unit cost. Costs are dominated by the shortage term, and the differences among the non-tuned arms (0.1--3.7\%) should be read with caution. The simulation-tuned policy is by far the cheapest (1{,}094{,}043 at one times the unit cost, about 33\% below SAP-derived), because it was selected to meet the service target on the pre-cutoff history. Among the other arms SAP-derived parameters are the cheapest, as they carry more inventory and fewer stockouts. LLM run~1 then costs 2.4\% more than SAP-derived at both multiples, is about as expensive as the Croston/SBA OR (1{,}673{,}003 versus 1{,}674{,}925 at one times), and is cheaper than the SLT-informed (1.2\%), ERP-floor, empirical-quantile and history-only (0.8\%) ORs. The LLM's cost advantage over ERP-derived parameters is therefore specific to the case in which service shortfalls are not charged.

\section{Managerial Insights}
\label{sec:managerial}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Position the tool as a reviewed decision support system, not an autonomous engine.} Single generations differ at the material level (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}). The gate-simulate-review structure yields a justified candidate for every material and leaves the decision to the planner; whether this helps planners is untested.
\item \textbf{Benchmark against data-driven baselines.} ERP-derived parameters and ERP-informed ORs are weak benchmarks (safety stocks are often stale or zero; safety lead times inflate buffers), so comparing only with ERP overestimates the benefit: the LLM cost 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile baselines.
\item \textbf{Expect plant-level differences.} Against ERP-derived parameters the LLM was cheaper in Plants A and B (14--23\%) and on 33 lumpy materials (about 25\%, mixed service) but not in Plant C; we did not test whether escalation rules would catch such cases.
\item \textbf{Verify storage along the trajectory.} The static rule $SS+Q\le\mathrm{limit}$ cannot protect a warehouse: 53--66\% of pairs breach the limit in simulation under each of the three arms checked. Enforce a cap in the order-release logic or treat the limit as a design requirement.
\item \textbf{Price shortages explicitly.} Without a shortage penalty the ranking rewards under-stocking; with a penalty of one unit cost the LLM is about 2.4\% more expensive than SAP-derived. Set service targets and penalties before reading cost comparisons.
\item \textbf{Freeze and log the configuration.} Treat the system prompt as a controlled file, log every call (input hash, time, session, raw response) and re-run before changing the prompt.
\item \textbf{Cost and latency.} Each generation took several minutes, whereas OR baselines take milliseconds; periodic regeneration would support adaptable planning \cite{sengupta2025uncertainty}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Research Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
A prompt-configured LLM generates feasible, reviewable $(r,Q)$ policies for every material; in simulation they cost less than ERP-derived parameters but more than data-driven OR policies. InventOR is therefore not presented as outperforming optimization: its role is a reviewed candidate generator, and whether that helps planners is a hypothesis the simulation cannot test. Against the ERP-informed arms alone the LLM would look far better ($-11.4\%$ against SAP-derived, $-30.5\%$ against the SLT-informed OR).

\paragraph{Design principles for LLM-based decision support.}
We derive six principles from this application and offer them as hypotheses for design-science work \cite{hevner2004design}. \textbf{DP1, bound the generator:} the LLM is a candidate generator whose prompt fixes the method, constraints and output schema, with no authority to act. \textbf{DP2, verify deterministically and along time:} check outputs with code and feasibility along the trajectory, because point checks can pass while constraints are violated over time (Section~\ref{subsec:capacity}). \textbf{DP3, show counterfactual evidence:} give the reviewer simulated comparisons with credible references. \textbf{DP4, control information in evaluation:} compare against baselines with and without the same information and withhold incumbent inputs in an ablation (Sections~\ref{subsec:arms-results}, \ref{subsec:ablation}). \textbf{DP5, freeze and log:} treat the prompt as a controlled file, log every call and repeat runs (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}). \textbf{DP6, escalate by rule:} define in advance when proposals are trusted, escalated or rejected, to limit complacency \cite{parasuraman2010complacency} (Section~\ref{subsec:rules}).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) The data come from one manufacturer and three plants. (ii) The model identifier and inference settings are operator-declared. (iii) No planner outcomes were measured: the benefits of reviewability, auditability and planner support and the rules of Section~\ref{subsec:rules} are design intentions that a field study must test. (iv) The simulation uses deterministic arrival at the rounded mean lead time, ignores observed receipts, corrections and scrap, starts with an empty pipeline and does not simulate lead-time variability, which may bias comparisons that depend on safety lead time; we did not report service-target attainment. (v) The primary results use a zero shortage penalty; with a positive penalty the ranking changes. Costs are dominated by a few high-demand materials and the 346 pairs are not independent, so the bootstrap intervals understate uncertainty. (vi) The $(r,Q)$ family is single-item and single-echelon. (vii) Individual policies vary across runs, and Plant C required calendar reconstruction. (viii) We included no deep reinforcement-learning or neural forecasting baseline, and the ERP-derived arm (ERP safety stock with our own order quantity) is not an observed record of practice.

\paragraph{Research perspectives.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Ensembling and hybrids.} Aggregating several generations may reduce individual-policy noise \cite{nakhost2025copilot}, and combining the best data-driven OR policy with LLM review or regime-specific choice \cite{wasserkrug2025enhancing} could capture the OR cost level and the LLM's flags.
\item \emph{Comparison with trained methods.} Benchmarks against deep reinforcement-learning and other trained policies \cite{gabellini2026novel, kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl} on identical data and information would clarify the trade-off between setup effort and policy quality.
\item \emph{Richer policy families.} $(s,S)$ policies and regime-specific estimators may help lumpy and Plant C pairs \cite{prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}.
\item \emph{Planner-in-the-loop field trials.} Prospective studies should compare planners with and without the aid on matched materials, measuring planner time, override rates and realized service and inventory, and should examine adoption drivers \cite{venkatesh2023adoption, cannas2023aiscm, trifone2026agentic}.
\item \emph{Standards for evaluation.} LLM-based decision support studies should report an incumbent-independent baseline, an ablation withholding incumbent inputs, trajectory-level feasibility and run-to-run variation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We defined InventOR as a human-reviewed decision support system for a recurring production-planning decision, the setting of replenishment parameters per plant-material pair, and evaluated it against six design requirements on a three-plant industrial dataset. A prompt-configured LLM generated feasible, justified $(r,Q)$ policies for every material without training, and a deterministic gate, a hard-limit simulation and explicit review rules made them checkable. The proposals cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at slightly lower service, but the LLM costs about 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile policies; when lost sales are priced at one unit cost or more, ERP-derived parameters are the cheapest of the non-tuned arms and a simulation-tuned policy is cheapest overall. Storage limits that hold in a static check are exceeded along simulated trajectories for every method, and the LLM loses to ERP at one plant. The evidence supports InventOR as a checkable candidate generator inside a reviewed process, not as an optimizer that beats data-driven baselines; the design principles of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} are hypotheses, and field evidence on planner behaviour and realized outcomes is the central open step.

\section{Formal Definitions}
\label{app:math}

\paragraph{Reference calculation.}
These are the standard textbook calculations \cite{silver2017inventory, axsater2015inventory}. For pair $i$, let $\bar d_i$ and $\sigma_{D,i}$ be the mean and standard deviation of pre-cutoff working-day demand, $L_i$ and $\sigma_{L,i}$ the mean and standard deviation of the lead time in working days, $K_i$ the ordering cost, $h_i$ the annual holding cost per unit (unit cost times holding rate), $z_i$ the standard normal quantile of the service target, and $250$ the working days per year. The order quantity is $Q_i=\max\{\mathrm{MOQ}_i,\lceil\sqrt{2K_i\cdot250\bar d_i/h_i}\,\rceil\}$. The lead-time-demand standard deviation is $\sigma_{LTD,i}=\sqrt{L_i\sigma_{D,i}^2+\bar d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2}$, the safety stock $SS_i=\lceil z_i\sigma_{LTD,i}\rceil$ and the reorder point $R_i=\lceil\bar d_iL_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+SS_i\rceil$, where $L^{\mathrm{eff}}_i$ is the mean lead time for the history-only OR and the mean lead time plus the ERP safety lead time for the SLT-informed OR and the ERP-floor OR. The SAP-derived arm uses the ERP safety stock and the same $Q_i$. All policies are projected to $SS_i+Q_i\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ when $\mathrm{MOQ}_i+SS_i$ fits.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
On each post-cutoff working day, demand is served from on-hand stock, unmet demand is lost, and an order of size $Q_i$ is placed when the inventory position (on-hand plus on order) is at or below $R_i$ and arrives after the rounded mean lead time in subsequent working observations. Observed receipts, corrections and scrap are ignored. The total cost is $TC_i=h_i\bar I_iT/250+K_iN_i$, where $\bar I_i$ is the mean on-hand stock over the $T$ simulated working days and $N_i$ the number of orders. Fill rate is served divided by demanded units. In the hard-limit replay an order is truncated to $\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ minus the inventory position, and no order is placed if the truncated quantity is zero.
