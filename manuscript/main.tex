\begin{abstract}
Material planners must set a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity for thousands of plant-material pairs, usually under ERP parameters that were last revised long ago. We define and evaluate \emph{InventOR}, a decision support system (DSS) for this recurring decision. For each material, a large language model (LLM) configured only through a prompt, without training or fine-tuning, proposes a reorder-point/order-quantity $(r,Q)$ policy with a written justification and an escalation flag. Deterministic software gates the proposal against minimum order quantities and storage limits and replays it on held-out demand before the planner, who remains the decision-maker, reviews it. We derive six design requirements and evaluate the artifact on 365 plant-material pairs from three plants of an industrial manufacturer, using three independent LLM runs, seven reference arms and an ablation that withholds the ERP planning parameters. In a hard-limit storage replay, the LLM policies cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at a mean fill rate 0.1--0.4 points lower, but 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile $(r,Q)$ baselines. The saving comes from carrying 13--17\% less inventory with 11--20\% more stockout days; when shortages are priced at one unit cost, ERP-derived parameters are cheaper and a simulation-tuned policy is cheapest. Static storage checks are insufficient: simulated trajectories exceed storage limits for 53--66\% of pairs. InventOR is a checkable, human-reviewed candidate generator, not an optimizer that beats data-driven baselines; benefits to planners are untested. We contribute design principles, review rules and a research agenda.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Replenishment parameters in manufacturing are set per plant-material pair: a reorder point that triggers an order, a safety stock that absorbs demand and lead-time variability, and an order quantity. In large ERP systems these values often remain at levels set years earlier, while demand patterns, supplier performance and storage constraints change. Revisiting them materially requires analysts who can fit demand and lead-time statistics, apply inventory theory, respect minimum order quantities (MOQs) and storage limits, and explain the result to planners. That scarce skill is exactly what a conversational large language model (LLM) with a code interpreter appears to offer, and a rapidly growing literature explores such tools for supply-chain and operations decisions \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, trifone2026agentic}.

The promise is real, but so are three problems that limit its use in practice. First, LLM outputs can be unfaithful or fabricated \cite{ji2023survey}, so a proposed policy must be treated as a candidate and not a decision. Second, claims of superiority over ERP parameters are hard to interpret when the LLM and the comparators are given different information; an LLM that sees a plant's ERP reference values and is compared with an OR policy that does not, or the reverse, tells us little about the LLM. Third, inventory policies live inside physical limits: a storage rule that holds when the policy is written down can be violated as stock builds up over time.

This paper treats the problem as one of decision support system (DSS) design. We define \emph{InventOR} as a human-reviewed, model-driven DSS for the recurring decision of setting replenishment parameters per plant-material pair (Section~\ref{sec:scope}). A planner remains the decision-maker. The system supplies, for each material, a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy proposed by an enterprise LLM application that is configured only through a prompt and receives no project-specific training or fine-tuning, together with a justification, an escalation flag and a counterfactual simulation; deterministic software parses, gates and simulates every proposal before the planner reviews it and alone decides whether any ERP parameter is changed. The pattern is not specific to inventory: any production-planning decision in which parameters are set per item and refreshed periodically (lot sizes, planned lead times, buffer levels, dispatching rules) has the same structure of many semi-structured decisions, scarce analyst time, hard constraints and a need for accountable evidence. The design lessons below therefore concern LLM-based decision support for production and logistics planning in general, and not only inventory control.

We follow a design-science logic \cite{hevner2004design, peffers2007design}: we state the problem and its scope, derive design requirements, build the artifact, and evaluate it against those requirements on real industrial data. Four research questions structure the paper.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Design.} How should an LLM be embedded in a DSS so that its proposals are bounded, verifiable, reproducible and reviewable by a planner?
\item \emph{Efficacy.} What cost and service do the proposed policies achieve relative to ERP-derived parameters and to OR policies, when the information available to each approach is controlled?
\item \emph{Feasibility and reliability.} Do the policies respect physical storage limits along simulated inventory trajectories, and how stable are they across independent runs?
\item \emph{Use.} Under which conditions should a planner trust, escalate or reject a proposal?
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{A definition and scope of the DSS} (Section~\ref{sec:scope}): the decision, the decision-maker, the system boundary, the level of automation and six design requirements that make the role of the LLM explicit and bounded.
\item \textbf{A DSS architecture and operating rules} (Section~\ref{sec:aid}) that separate generation, deterministic gating, simulation and planner review, with explicit rules for when output is trusted, escalated or rejected, and design principles derived from this application (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\item \textbf{An information-controlled, trajectory-level evaluation} (Section~\ref{sec:design}) with seven reference arms that differ in the information they use and in method (ERP-derived, ERP-informed, normal-approximation, Croston/SBA, empirical-quantile and simulation-tuned policies), an LLM ablation denied the ERP planning parameters, and a hard-limit storage replay that is the primary result. This separates what the LLM adds from what it is given.
\item \textbf{Complete-cohort industrial evidence} from three LLM runs on 365 plant-material pairs, with plant-level, regime-level and sensitivity analyses, and managerial and research implications.
\end{enumerate}

Our main findings are as follows. Against ERP-derived parameters the LLM policies are about 11\% cheaper at a mean fill rate 0.1--0.4 points lower, but the LLM costs 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile $(r,Q)$ baselines at essentially equal service, and its saving over ERP comes from carrying less inventory at the price of more stockout days. Storage limits that hold in a static check are violated along unconstrained trajectories for 53--66\% of pairs. The simulation therefore does not show superior optimization by the LLM; it shows that a prompt-configured LLM can produce checkable candidate policies without code or training, whose benefit to planners remains to be tested.


\section{Problem, Scope and Definition}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{The decision problem}
\label{subsec:decision}
The decision supported is the periodic (re)setting of three replenishment parameters, a reorder point, a safety stock and an order quantity, for each plant-material pair held in an ERP system. In the classification of Gorry and Scott Morton \cite{gorry1971framework} it is an operational-control decision that is partly structured: the calculation is textbook, but the choices of estimator, the treatment of intermittent or trending demand, the plausibility of recorded lead times and the handling of constraints require judgement that is applied inconsistently when thousands of materials compete for a few analysts' time. The decision-maker is the material planner or inventory analyst; the decision is repeated, high-volume and consequential for working capital, service and storage. Hard constraints are the minimum order quantity (MOQ) and a physical storage limit. The feedback loop is slow: a changed parameter shows its effect only in subsequent demand.

\subsection{Definition and level of automation}
\label{subsec:definition}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Definition.} \emph{InventOR is a human-reviewed, model-driven decision support system that, for one plant-material pair at a time, (i) obtains from a large language model, configured only by a written prompt, a candidate $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; (ii) verifies the candidate deterministically against explicit constraints; (iii) evaluates it by counterfactual simulation against reference policies on held-out demand; and (iv) presents the result to a planner, who alone decides whether ERP parameters are changed.}
\end{quote}
In the classical DSS architecture of data, model and dialog components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, the LLM and the simulator form the model component, the material-scoped data extract and the prompt form the data and knowledge components, and the review interface is the dialog component (Section~\ref{subsec:architecture}). In terms of the levels of automation of Parasuraman et al. \cite{parasuraman2000model}, InventOR operates at a low-to-intermediate level: it selects and suggests one alternative and leaves execution to the human. This is a design decision, not a limitation: acceptance of automated suggestions is accompanied by complacency and automation bias \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}, which the review rules of Section~\ref{subsec:rules} are meant to counter.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{subsec:scope}
InventOR covers the periodic review of reorder point, safety stock and order quantity for one plant-material pair at a time (single item, single echelon, continuous review, lost sales, $(r,Q)$ family). Order release, supplier selection, pricing, forecasting-model development, multi-item coupling and multi-echelon networks are out of scope. The LLM is a candidate generator with a justification and an escalation flag; it does not forecast, place orders, run the simulation or take the final decision, which stays with the planner. The evaluation is an ex-post simulation on real post-cutoff demand with information-controlled comparison, hard storage-limit replay and repeated runs; field trials, planner workload, decision quality and user acceptance are outside it.

\subsection{Design requirements}
\label{subsec:requirements}
Four sources motivate the requirements: the known unreliability of LLM outputs \cite{ji2023survey}, guidance on human--AI interaction and accountability \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, the run-to-run nondeterminism of LLM inference \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}, and the practical constraints of inventory control. They yield six requirements (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}), each tied to a design feature (Section~\ref{sec:aid}) and an evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Research approach}
\label{subsec:approach}
We follow the design-science research process of problem identification, requirements, artifact design and evaluation \cite{hevner2004design, peffers2007design}. In the evaluation framework of Venable et al. \cite{venable2016feds}, our evaluation is artificial and ex post: it uses real industrial data and a faithful simulation but no live users. It therefore establishes technical efficacy, feasibility and reliability; it does not establish usability, adoption or improved planner decisions, which we list as limitations and research directions.


\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Decision support systems and human--AI decision making.}
DSS research has long distinguished data-, model- and knowledge-driven systems and has stressed that the value of a DSS lies in improving the judgement of a human decision-maker rather than in replacing it \cite{gorry1971framework, sprague1980framework, shim2002past}. LLMs add a new kind of model component: it is configured through language, produces free-text justification and is not deterministic. Research on human--automation interaction shows that the way suggestions are scoped, presented and verified determines whether users rely on them appropriately or over-rely on them \cite{parasuraman2000model, parasuraman2010complacency}, and guidelines for human--AI interaction call for explicit scoping, explanation and correction mechanisms \cite{amershi2019guidelines}. Our contribution to this stream is a concrete, evaluated instance of an LLM-based DSS for an operational production decision, with design requirements that are tied to evidence. Recent DSS work derives design principles for LLM-based assistants \cite{schelhorn2026design}, reports design and pilot implementations of AI-enabled decision support in operations \cite{prithvirajan2026aidss}, and shows that the uptake of algorithmic advice depends on transparency and on the user's metacognition \cite{ning2024transparency, soleimanof2026metacognition}; evaluations of data-driven forecasting systems in practice likewise stress lessons beyond accuracy \cite{chae2024restaurant}. Model-driven decision support is also known to shape how groups handle disagreement \cite{engin2024conflict}. Human override of algorithmic safety-stock advice is a documented risk in inventory settings \cite{wang2024aversion}, which is why InventOR keeps the planner as decision-maker and makes the evidence for each proposal explicit.

\paragraph{LLMs in operations and supply-chain decisions.}
Reviews and frameworks map the opportunities for generative AI in supply chains \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, li2024genaiscm, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, boone2025generative} and stress that adoption depends on assigned human roles, data quality and governance \cite{trifone2026agentic, cannas2023aiscm, venkatesh2023adoption, cohen2026supply, olan2025xai}. Case evidence from manufacturing planning is still thin: examples include a fine-tuned LLM for digital-manufacturing optimization \cite{amarasinghe2026finetuned} and a transformer trained to set order timing and quantity, benchmarked against $(r,Q)$ \cite{liu2026transformer}; experiments with managers also show that ChatGPT reproduces human decision biases \cite{chen2025manager}. A recent conceptual line proposes agentic digital twins that combine AI agents with model-based simulation and optimization to support decisions \cite{ivanov2026agentic}, and an LLM-plus-digital-twin method has been built for closed-loop supply-chain operations \cite{le2026llmdt}. Our decision support system follows the same logic at a smaller scale: an LLM proposes and a simulation model checks. Most technical LLM work focuses on translating natural-language problem statements into optimization models and solver code \cite{li2023large, huang2025orlm, ahmaditeshnizi2024optimus, zhang2025or}, on multi-agent control and consensus-seeking in inventory flows \cite{quan2024invagent, jannelli2025agentic}, on extracting supply-network structure from text \cite{almahri2025kgllm, zheng2025visibility}, on disruption modelling \cite{lin2025unisone}, or on evaluating LLMs on classical operations-research tasks such as vehicle routing \cite{hamdan2026routing}. The closest LLM study to ours uses an LLM as a forecasting copilot for spare-parts demand, combined with simulation, and reports high variability for irregular demand that can be reduced by averaging repeated queries and enriching prompts \cite{nakhost2025copilot}. We share the concern about run-to-run variability and test it directly (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}), but differ in that the LLM proposes a complete replenishment policy rather than a forecast.

\paragraph{Data-driven inventory control without LLMs.}
Learning-based replenishment is well established, including data-driven policies learned from sequentially observed non-stationary demand \cite{ren2024datadriven} and forecasts augmented with leading indicators \cite{sagaert2025leading}, and heuristics remain competitive in constrained settings such as perishable inventory \cite{gulecyuz2025heuristic}. Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to omnichannel and multi-echelon systems \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl, ma2026omnichannel} and aerospace replenishment \cite{wang2022aerospace}; deep-learning forecasting has been integrated with replenishment \cite{ma2026integrated}; contextual distributionally robust optimization uses side information to set order quantities \cite{wang2026contextual}; and distribution-free online algorithms handle capacitated warehouses under uncertain price and demand \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. Robust inventory control under demand and lead-time uncertainty has a longer history \cite{bertsimas2006robust, thorsen2017robust, zhang2025robust}, and lead-time variability and dependence are known to propagate through ordering decisions \cite{disney2025bullwhip}, while safety stock and safety lead time are alternative buffers against unreliability \cite{vanKampen2010safety}. The closest operational neighbour is a trained data-driven framework that jointly sets replenishment parameters, safety stock and safety lead time on real automotive data \cite{gabellini2026novel}. Reinforcement learning has also been combined with demand forecasting to optimize order points \cite{schett2026rlorder}. These methods require model training, historical scenario generation or tuned hyperparameters for each setting; InventOR requires none, at the price of an LLM-based generator whose internal logic cannot be inspected beyond its written justification.

\paragraph{Intermittent and spare-parts demand.}
Material histories in manufacturing are frequently intermittent or lumpy. The statistical literature offers specialized estimators and classification schemes \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy, willemain2004new, prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, and recent studies develop feature-based forecast combinations and temporal aggregation for intermittent demand \cite{sanguri2023intermittent, li2022featurebased}, forecasting for spare parts across product life-cycle phases \cite{goldsmith2025spare}, and integrated forecasting and planning or multi-echelon stocking models for repairable and spare parts \cite{babaveisi2022repairable, caserta2026amazon}. Related industrial studies examine how manufacturers identify and select safety buffers \cite{hedvall2025buffers}. Our regime analysis (Section~\ref{subsec:plants}) shows where an LLM that is forbidden from using specialized intermittent-demand estimators behaves differently on lumpy materials.

In summary, InventOR is not a new inventory model and does not claim to beat trained or optimized methods. It contributes (i) a DSS design in which an off-the-shelf LLM is a bounded candidate generator inside a reviewed process, (ii) an evaluation that controls the information available to each arm, and (iii) industrial evidence on cost, service and storage feasibility. The $(r,Q)$ policy it uses is the standard workhorse of continuous-review control \cite{hadley1963analysis, porteus2002foundations, silver2017inventory}.

\section{The InventOR Decision Support System}
\label{sec:aid}

\subsection{Architecture and components}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Following the classical DSS components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, InventOR has a \emph{data} component (a material-scoped extract of daily demand, lead times, working-day flags, cost and constraint parameters and ERP reference values; the fields supplied are controlled and logged; DR4), a \emph{knowledge} component (the system prompt file defining the task, the prohibited methods, the storage rules and the output schema, Supplementary File S1; DR1, DR5), a \emph{model} component split into a generator (an enterprise LLM application with a code interpreter, configured only by the prompt, proposing one $(r,Q)$ policy with a justification and an escalation flag; DR1) and a deterministic verifier and simulator (parser, gate, held-out simulation with hard storage-limit replay and reference arms; DR2, DR3), a \emph{dialog} component (policy, justification, flag and simulated comparison shown to the planner under the trust, escalate and reject rules; DR6) and a \emph{governance} layer (frozen prompt file, per-call log of input hash, time, session and raw response, repeated runs; DR5). The generator is the only component whose internal logic is not inspectable; it is surrounded by deterministic and auditable components. In the evaluated prototype the dialog component is the per-pair output record and the comparison tables; an interactive planner interface was not built or evaluated.

\subsection{Operation in four steps}
A planner's question is: \emph{given what this material has done in the past, how much safety stock should I hold, at what stock level should I reorder, and how much should I order?} InventOR answers this question in four steps (Figure~\ref{fig:tool_process_map}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{Figure_1_Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{InventOR workflow: material data are prepared, an LLM proposes a policy, deterministic software parses, checks and simulates it, and a planner reviews the result.}
  \label{fig:tool_process_map}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Generate.} The LLM, accessed through an enterprise application with a code interpreter, receives one material's history (daily demand, realized lead times, working-day flags) and a short parameter block (unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target, storage limit, and current ERP safety stock and safety lead time for comparison). A written prompt (the only configuration; no weights are changed) \cite{sahoo2024systematic} instructs it to compute demand and lead-time statistics, classify the demand pattern, apply an economic-order-quantity (EOQ) argument and a normal-approximation safety stock, respect the storage limit, and report a structured policy together with a short justification and escalation flag.
\item \textbf{Gate.} Deterministic code extracts the policy, rejects malformed or infeasible outputs (missing or non-numeric fields, non-positive reorder point or order quantity, negative safety stock, reorder point below safety stock, safety stock plus order quantity above the storage limit) and retries the generation up to three times. The backtest applies one further admission check, that the order quantity is not below the MOQ.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Each accepted policy is replayed on the material's demand observed \emph{after} the data cutoff, a held-out evaluation in the sense of out-of-sample forecast testing \cite{tashman2000out, hewamalage2023forecast}, together with the comparator policies, producing holding and ordering cost, fill rate and stockout days. This plays the role that model-based simulation plays in digital-twin decision support \cite{ivanov2026agentic}, at the scale of a single policy.
\item \textbf{Review.} A planner sees the policy, the LLM's justification and flag, and the simulated comparison. Only the planner changes ERP parameters.
\end{enumerate}

The role of the LLM is deliberately narrow. It is a \emph{candidate generator} whose output is never trusted without the gate, and it is not used to forecast, to place orders, or to run the simulation. The prompt prohibits Monte Carlo simulation, distribution fitting, machine-learning libraries and specialized intermittent-demand estimators, so that the policy rests on transparent statistics a planner can reproduce.

\subsection{Trust, review and reject rules}
\label{subsec:rules}
We propose three explicit rules for planners, in line with guidelines for human--AI interaction and with internal-auditing practice for AI systems \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, and motivated by the evidence in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The rules and the 0.5-point threshold are design proposals that we did not evaluate with planners; with the baselines used here the trust rule would be met by only a minority of pairs (the LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in 74--77 of 346), which is itself a finding about the LLM.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[Trust (apply after review)] when the proposal passes the gate, the simulated trajectory respects the storage limit when orders are truncated at it, and the simulated cost is not above that of the best history-only OR reference (Croston/SBA or empirical quantile) with a fill rate not more than about 0.5 points lower.
\item[Escalate] when the LLM raises an escalation flag, the material is lumpy or has strongly seasonal or trending demand, the plant or calendar data were repaired, or the policy differs strongly between repeated generations.
\item[Reject] when the proposal fails the gate after the retry budget, exceeds the storage limit in the hard-limit replay, or is dominated (higher cost and lower fill) by a history-only reference.
\end{description}

\subsection{Provenance and reproducibility of the configuration}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The system prompt, which fully defines the task, the forbidden methods, the capacity rules and the output schema, is a plain-text file that is deployed unchanged in the enterprise application; it is distributed with the paper (Supplementary File S1) and the code repository. Every call logs the SHA-256 of its input data, the UTC time, the application session identifier and the full raw response. The platform does not expose the underlying model identifier or sampling settings to the client; the evaluated model (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6, with a code interpreter) is therefore declared by the operator and is not machine-verifiable. We report this limitation openly and base the reproducibility argument on the prompt file, the per-call logs and three independent runs (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}) rather than on a model checkpoint.

\section{Application and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Evaluation approach}
\label{subsec:evalapproach}
The evaluation addresses each design requirement in turn (Table~\ref{tab:requirements}): whether the pipeline returns valid, feasible policies (DR1, DR2); what cost and service the policies achieve against reference arms on held-out demand (DR3); how much of that result is due to the information given to the LLM rather than to its reasoning (DR4); whether storage limits hold along the simulated trajectory (DR2); how stable policies are across independent runs (DR5); and where proposals should be escalated (DR6). The primary basis is the hard-limit replay with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty; sensitivity analyses vary these choices. The evaluation is ex post and uses no live users (Section~\ref{subsec:approach}).

\subsection{Industrial application and data}\label{subsec:data}
The application is a pilot at a large manufacturer. The dataset contains 233{,}078 daily records for 411 plant-material pairs from three plants (A, B and C), with a data cutoff on 10 March 2026 and a post-cutoff horizon observed to 27 August 2026. Of the 411 pairs, 365 are eligible for scoring (234 in Plant A, 65 in Plant B, 66 in Plant C) and are sent to the LLM. For 19 pairs (all in Plant A) the supplied MOQ plus the reference safety stock exceeds the storage limit, so no comparator can satisfy both rules; these are excluded from outcome comparison, leaving a \emph{common-feasible cohort} of 346 pairs (215, 65 and 66). All 365 pairs receive an LLM policy. Demand is concentrated: the five largest materials account for 40.3\% of demand and the ten largest for 57.8\%. The source working-day flag of Plant C is all zero; we reconstruct it from the same-date majority of Plants A and B (ties count as working days) and test the effect of this repair in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}. Such data-quality problems are a known threat to analytics-based decision support \cite{watts2009data} and, for inventory systems, to record accuracy \cite{dehoratius2008inventory, kang2005information, mersereau2013information}.

Each pair is sent as a material-scoped CSV with daily demand, recorded lead times, a working-day flag, the minimum order quantity, unit cost, holding-cost rate, ordering cost, service-level target, the storage limit, and two \emph{ERP planning parameters} (current safety stock and safety lead time), which are withheld in the ablation arm. The CSV also carries supplier and lane context fields that the prompt does not use.

\subsection{Reference arms and information content}
\label{subsec:arms}
The LLM and the classical comparators do not necessarily use the same information, and this matters for the interpretation of any cost difference, because ERP safety stock and safety lead time embed accumulated planner judgement, and safety stock and safety lead time are alternative ways of buffering unreliability \cite{vanKampen2010safety}. Planned lead times can also drift upward when they are adjusted dynamically (the ``lead time syndrome'' \cite{thurer2022planned}), which would inflate any comparator that adds them to the lead time. We therefore construct arms that make the information explicit (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

Three further arms use no ERP input and represent stronger data-driven practice than the history-only OR. The \emph{Croston/SBA OR} replaces the mean daily demand by the Croston estimate with the Syntetos--Boylan bias correction \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy} (smoothing constant 0.1, working-day demand history), uses the root mean squared one-step forecast error as demand spread and the history-only lead-time moments, which suits intermittent demand \cite{willemain2004new}. The \emph{empirical-quantile OR} sets the reorder point to the service-level quantile of the historical lead-time demand, computed from rolling sums over the rounded mean lead time in working days, and needs no distributional assumption. The \emph{simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$} policy is a learned baseline: from a grid of reserve factors and order-quantity multiples around the history-only policy it selects the cheapest candidate whose fill rate on the last 250 working days \emph{before the cutoff} meets the service target (or the best-fill candidate if none does). No post-cutoff data are used in any of these arms. We do not implement a deep reinforcement-learning baseline \cite{stranieri2024drl, kolyaei2025rl}; the simulation-tuned policy is the trained comparator, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists neural baselines as a limitation.

\subsection{Simulation, capacity replay and metrics}
Each pair is simulated independently on the post-cutoff horizon as a single-item, single-echelon continuous-review $(r,Q)$ system \cite{hadley1963analysis, axsater2015inventory, zipkin2000foundations, silver2017inventory, porteus2002foundations} with lost sales \cite{bijvank2011lost}, in the spirit of simulation-based experiments on supply and timing uncertainty \cite{grasso1984simulation}, a shared opening stock, an empty pipeline and arrivals after the rounded mean lead time in working days (Appendix~\ref{app:math}). MOQs are treated as hard order-size constraints, as imposed by suppliers \cite{heese2025moq}, and storage limits as capacity bounds on the replenishment decision \cite{zhang2023replenishment}. The primary metrics are the mean material-horizon holding-plus-ordering cost, the mean material-level fill rate, the aggregate (volume-weighted) fill rate and the number of stockout days. We report paired differences with 10{,}000-sample bootstrap 95\% intervals.

A static check $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$ does not show that storage limits are respected as the inventory evolves, because the reorder point includes lead-time demand and inventory on order can exceed the limit when it arrives. We therefore record, for every policy and every simulated day, the post-receipt on-hand stock and report the share of pairs and days above the limit (the \emph{unconstrained} simulation). We then \emph{replay} every policy with each order truncated to the storage limit minus the current inventory position (the \emph{hard-limit replay}). The hard-limit replay is our primary evaluation.

\subsection{Repeated runs and sensitivities}
To examine run-to-run variation, the entire cohort is generated three times (runs 1, 2 and 3) with the same prompt file and the same data. Run~1 serves as the reference for tabulated results unless stated otherwise; runs 2 and 3 provide replicates. Sensitivity analyses vary the working-day reconstruction (majority, any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals), the shortage penalty (zero, one and five times unit cost).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Completion and policy characteristics}\label{subsec:completion}
All four runs (three with the full input and one ablation, Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) return a valid, storage-feasible $(r,Q)$ policy for all 365 pairs after at most three attempts per pair. The validity gate rejects non-positive reorder points and order quantities, negative safety stocks and reorder points below the safety stock. The ERP safety stock is zero for 198 of the 365 pairs (188 of the 346 in the cohort below), so for a majority of pairs the SAP-derived arm holds no safety stock at all.

\subsection{Headline comparison with ERP-derived parameters}\label{subsec:headline}
Unless stated otherwise, all results from here on use the hard-limit replay (every order truncated at the storage limit) on the 346-pair common-feasible cohort, with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty; Section~\ref{subsec:capacity} reports the unconstrained simulation for comparison. Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results} compares all arms, including the LLM cost relative to each arm in the three runs (positive values mean that the LLM costs more). In run~1 the LLM policies cost 11.4\% less than SAP-derived policies and 30.5\% less than the SLT-informed OR comparator (mean 2{,}928 versus 3{,}305 and 4{,}211). The mean fill rate is 0.18 points lower than for SAP-derived (98.11\% versus 98.30\%; bootstrap interval $-0.54$ to $+0.10$) and 0.11 points lower than for the SLT-informed OR. The mean cost difference against SAP-derived is $-377$ (95\% interval $-651$ to $-154$); the LLM is cheaper than SAP-derived in 241 of 346 pairs and, in 226 pairs, simultaneously no more expensive and no worse in fill. The LLM incurs more stockout days in total (910 versus 804 and 831), and aggregate fill is 78.87\% versus 79.34\% and 79.15\%.

The cost decomposition shows where the saving comes from. Relative to SAP-derived parameters the LLM carries about 17\% less average inventory (16{,}256 versus 19{,}565 units) and has about 16\% lower holding cost, while it places about the same number of orders per year (17.3 versus 17.6). The saving is therefore a trade of inventory for service: the LLM holds less stock and accepts 11--20\% more stockout days (runs 3 and 2). It is not the consequence of a better ordering rhythm, and it is not free of service cost. Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that, once shortages carry a cost, this trade no longer favours the LLM.

\subsection{Information asymmetry: what the LLM adds beyond the ERP inputs}
\label{subsec:arms-results}
The comparison below uses Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results}. Three results stand out.

First, the SLT-informed OR and its ERP-floor variant are the most expensive arms (4{,}211 and 4{,}248). The ERP safety lead time inflates their effective lead time, hence their buffers; against them the LLM is about 30--31\% cheaper in every run. This comparison therefore mainly measures how heavily ERP inputs over-buffer, not the quality of the LLM.

Second, the \emph{history-only OR}, which uses no ERP inputs, has a cost of 2{,}764 at a fill of 98.14\%. Against it the LLM costs 5.9--6.0\% more in all three runs (run 1: mean difference $+164$, 95\% interval $+91$ to $+240$), with fill differences of $-0.03$, $-0.25$ and $+0.01$ points whose intervals include zero in all three runs. The LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in only 74--77 of 346 pairs. The stronger data-driven arms widen the gap. Against the Croston/SBA OR (2{,}705, fill 98.30\%) the LLM costs 8.2--8.4\% more (run~1: mean difference $+223$, interval $+97$ to $+362$; the LLM is cheaper in 91--99 pairs), and against the empirical-quantile OR (2{,}489, fill 98.35\%) it costs 17.7--17.8\% more (mean difference $+439$, interval $+329$ to $+557$; the LLM is cheaper in 44--49 pairs) at a mean fill that is 0.24 points lower. Part of the LLM's lower cost relative to ERP-derived parameters therefore reflects the weakness of those benchmarks; the LLM does not match a data-driven $(r,Q)$ baseline.

The simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$ policy sits at a different operating point. It reaches an aggregate fill of 84.8\% with the lowest average inventory (11{,}914 units) but a lower mean fill (96.05\%) and many more, smaller stockouts (1{,}973 stockout days), because its tuning criterion is the fill of the last pre-cutoff year, not the post-cutoff horizon. Against it the LLM costs 10.3--10.4\% less in the zero-penalty cost, with a 2.1-point higher mean fill, but the paired interval of the cost difference includes zero ($-2{,}527$ to $+857$ in run~1). Once shortages are priced it becomes by far the cheapest arm (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}), so its low zero-penalty ranking is not a sign of a weak baseline.

Third, the LLM is nonetheless cheaper than ERP-derived parameters (11.3--11.4\%) at a mean fill that is lower by 0.14--0.40 points (the interval excludes zero only in run~2), so it improves on the ERP-derived reference. It does not reach the best classical baselines, which also yield a policy for every material at lower cost; the evidence therefore does not establish a cost advantage of the LLM over data-driven alternatives. Whether the LLM relies on the ERP planning parameters it is given is tested in the ablation of Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}.

\subsection{LLM without ERP planning parameters (ablation)}
\label{subsec:ablation}
In this ablation the LLM receives exactly the same material histories, cost parameters, MOQ, service target and storage limit as in runs 1--3, but the ERP safety stock and ERP safety lead time are blanked in the uploaded CSV and marked ``not supplied'' in the inline parameter block; the system prompt file is unchanged. It tests whether the LLM anchors on the ERP planning parameters it is given, and therefore whether the comparison with the ERP-informed arms is confounded by information. All 365 pairs returned a valid, storage-compliant policy.

\emph{Policy values.} Withholding the ERP parameters changes the LLM's policies only slightly beyond its ordinary run-to-run variation. The median ratios of safety stock and reorder point to run~1 are both 1.00 (346 common pairs). The reorder point differs by more than 10\% in 54.6\% of pairs, against 48.3\% between runs~1 and~3 and 48.0\% between runs~2 and~3 with the ERP parameters supplied; the safety stock differs by more than 10\% in 11.0\% of pairs, against 9.8\% and 9.5\%. The median absolute reorder-point difference is 128 units, against 90 and 84 between ERP-supplied runs. The mean ratio is slightly above one (1.04 for safety stock, 1.05 for the reorder point), so the LLM tends to buffer a little more when it has no ERP anchor.

\emph{Outcomes.} In the hard-limit replay the LLM without ERP parameters has a mean cost of 3{,}014 at a mean fill of 98.06\%, against 2{,}928--2{,}931 and 97.89--98.16\% in runs~1--3. It costs 8.8\% less than SAP-derived parameters (95\% interval of the mean difference $-566$ to $-61$; fill $-0.24$ points, interval including zero) and 28.4--29.1\% less than the SLT-informed and ERP-floor ORs, but it costs 9.0\% more than the history-only OR (interval $+134$ to $+387$; fill $-0.09$ points; cheaper in 91 of 346 pairs), 11.4\% more than the Croston/SBA OR and 21.1\% more than the empirical-quantile OR, and 7.7\% less than the simulation-tuned policy at its different operating point. Withholding the ERP parameters thus raises the LLM's cost by roughly 3 percentage points relative to the history-only OR and does not reverse any ranking.

\emph{By plant.} The pattern is the same as with ERP inputs: the LLM costs less than SAP-derived at Plants A (215 pairs, $-10.8\%$) and B (65 pairs, $-21.9\%$) and more at Plant C (66 pairs, $+41.6\%$ against $+36.2\%$ in run~1), so the Plant~C loss is not caused by reliance on ERP parameters.

\emph{Interpretation.} Two conclusions follow. First, the information-asymmetry concern (that the LLM benefits from ERP inputs) does not explain the LLM's performance: with the ERP parameters removed the LLM keeps most of its advantage over ERP-derived parameters, and the gap to the history-only OR widens only moderately. Second, the ERP parameters are of limited use to the LLM; most of its output appears to be determined by the demand and lead-time histories. One limitation is that the ablation tests only the two ERP planning fields; the LLM still sees unit cost, holding rate, ordering cost, MOQ, service target and storage limit, which all reference arms also use.

\subsection{Storage limits along simulated trajectories}
\label{subsec:capacity}
All 365 LLM policies satisfy the static rule $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$, yet in the unconstrained simulation the post-receipt on-hand stock exceeds the limit for 183 pairs (52.9\%) for the LLM (178--183 across the three runs; 10.2\% of simulated pair-days), 198 pairs (57.2\%) for SAP-derived and 230 pairs (66.5\%) for the SLT-informed OR; no pair starts above the limit. Exceedances arise because inventory on order and lead-time demand add to stock above $SS+Q$, so the static check is necessary but not sufficient for any method. Under the hard-limit replay no arm exceeds the limit by more than one unit. Enforcing it raises the LLM mean cost from 2{,}850 to 2{,}928 and SAP-derived from 3{,}184 to 3{,}305 (SLT-informed OR from 3{,}852 to 4{,}211), without changing the ranking, and we use the replay as the primary evaluation.

\subsection{Plants and demand regimes}
\label{subsec:plants}
The average hides a strong plant effect (Table~\ref{tab:plants}, hard-limit replay, run~1). The LLM costs 14.1\% less than SAP-derived in Plant A (cheaper in 179 of 215 pairs) and 22.7\% less in Plant B (54 of 65), but 36.2\% \emph{more} in Plant C, where it is cheaper in only 8 of 66 pairs. The Plant~C loss is similar in the other full runs ($+32.2\%$ and $+31.5\%$) and larger without the ERP parameters ($+41.6\%$). Plant C is also the plant whose calendar we repaired, but Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that the loss persists under every working-day reconstruction in which Plant C can be scored. Against the data-driven baselines the LLM costs more in every plant: 7.2\%, 3.6\% and 6.7\% more than the history-only OR in Plants A, B and C; 13.1\%, 2.3\% and 6.2\% more than the Croston/SBA OR (it is cheaper in 26 of 65 Plant B pairs and 20 of 66 Plant C pairs); and 20.8\%, 7.7\% and 29.5\% more than the empirical-quantile OR. Plant B is where the LLM comes closest. The SLT-informed and history-only ORs coincide in Plant C because the ERP safety lead time is zero for all 66 of its pairs.

By demand regime, the 33 lumpy materials show the largest cost reduction against SAP-derived ($-25.4\%$ in run~1; $-23.7\%$ to $-25.2\%$ in the other runs), whereas the 313 smooth materials show $-9.8\%$ at a 0.24-point lower mean fill. The service effect on lumpy materials is inconsistent (mean fill $+0.38$ points in run~1, $-0.29$ in run~2), the history-only OR costs 9\% less than the LLM there at a much lower aggregate fill, and the Croston/SBA OR costs 9.7\% less at a higher mean fill (94.55\% versus 93.12\%); only the empirical-quantile OR is slightly more expensive (1.2\%; the LLM is cheaper in 15 of 33 pairs). With 33 materials this is indicative only. By terciles of the coefficient of variation, the LLM costs 15.7\% less than SAP-derived at equal fill in the lowest tercile, 7.5\% less with a 0.68-point lower mean fill in the middle tercile, and 12.9\% less in the highest.

\subsection{Run-to-run stability}
\label{subsec:stability}
Across the three runs the aggregate results are very similar: the LLM cost relative to SAP-derived is $-11.4\%$, $-11.3\%$ and $-11.4\%$ in the hard-limit replay, and the distributions of policy values have similar medians (reorder point 1{,}584, 1{,}582 and 1{,}466; order quantity 2{,}737, 2{,}777 and 2{,}693; safety stock 683, 688 and 669; Figure~\ref{fig:three-run-reproducibility}). Individual policies, however, vary. Comparing runs 2 and 3 on the 365 pairs, all three policy values are identical for only 36 pairs; every policy resolves to the $(r,Q)$ family, and the median absolute differences are 81 units for the reorder point, 6 for the order quantity and 3 for the safety stock. The reorder point differs by more than 10\% in about 48\% of the pairs, and a few pairs vary strongly (for one material the reorder point was 14{,}882, 14{,}947 and 29{,}218 units in the three runs; for two others the safety stock was zero in one run and 660 or 1{,}075 units in the others). One generation per material is therefore a noisy estimate: aggregate behaviour is reproducible, individual values should be reviewed. Such non-determinism is a documented property of LLMs \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}
\paragraph{Plant C calendar.} Plant C's source working-day flag is all zero, which required reconstruction. We re-scored all policies under four alternatives: any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals. Where Plant C can be scored (any-plant and calendar-day arrivals), the LLM remains 31--35\% more expensive than SAP-derived there in the three full runs, and the cohort-wide advantage is $-10.8\%$ to $-11.1\%$ (primary calendar: $-11.3\%$ to $-11.4\%$). The strict and exclusion variants (280 pairs) drop Plant C and show a larger advantage ($-17.0\%$ to $-17.6\%$). The Plant C result is therefore not an artefact of the calendar repair; one residual risk is that the LLM saw Plant C demand series built on the repaired calendar.

\paragraph{Shortage penalty.} With a positive lost-sales penalty the zero-penalty cost ranking changes. Table~\ref{tab:penalty} gives mean total cost per pair (holding, ordering and shortage cost) in the hard-limit replay for penalties of one and five times the unit cost; the zero-penalty costs are in Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results}. Costs are dominated by the shortage term and the gaps between arms are small in relative terms, so the ordering among the non-tuned arms (differences of 0.1--3.7\%) should be read with caution. The simulation-tuned policy is by far the cheapest arm as soon as shortages are priced (1{,}094{,}043 at one times the unit cost, about 33\% below SAP-derived), because it was selected to meet the service target on the pre-cutoff history. Among the other arms the SAP-derived parameters are the cheapest, because they carry more inventory and fewer stockouts. Run~1 of the LLM then costs 2.4\% more than SAP-derived at both one and five times the unit cost; it is about as expensive as the Croston/SBA OR (1{,}673{,}003 versus 1{,}674{,}925 at one times the unit cost), and it is cheaper than the SLT-informed OR (1.2\% lower at both multiples), the ERP-floor OR, the empirical-quantile OR and the history-only OR (0.8\% lower). The LLM's cost advantage over ERP-derived parameters is therefore specific to the case in which service shortfalls are not charged.

\section{Managerial Insights}
\label{sec:managerial}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Position the tool as a reviewed decision support system, not an autonomous policy engine.} Single generations differ at the material level (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}). The gate-simulate-review structure (Section~\ref{subsec:rules}) yields a candidate policy for every material with a justification and leaves the decision to the planner; whether this helps planners in practice has not been tested.
\item \textbf{Benchmark against data-driven OR baselines before claiming value.} ERP-derived parameters and ERP-informed OR policies are weak benchmarks: safety stocks are often stale or zero and safety lead times inflate the effective lead time. A planning team that evaluates an LLM aid only against ERP will overestimate its benefit. History-only $(r,Q)$ baselines (normal approximation, Croston/SBA, empirical lead-time-demand quantile) are cheap to compute and the LLM cost 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than they did in our data, respectively; a policy tuned by simulation on the pre-cutoff history was cheapest once shortages were priced.
\item \textbf{Where the LLM was closer to the baselines.} Against ERP-derived parameters the LLM was cheaper in Plants A and B (14--23\%) and on the 33 lumpy materials (about 25\%, with mixed service effects). In Plant C, where the LLM lost, conditions such as repaired calendars, small samples or atypical lead-time behaviour may call for escalation; we did not test this.
\item \textbf{Verify storage along the trajectory.} The static rule $SS+Q\le\mathrm{limit}$ cannot protect a warehouse: 53--66\% of pairs breach the limit in simulation under each of the three arms checked. Operations should enforce a cap in the order-release logic (order-up-to or truncation), or treat the limit as a design requirement in the policy.
\item \textbf{Price shortages explicitly.} Without a shortage penalty the cost ranking rewards under-stocking. With a penalty of one unit cost the LLM's cost advantage over SAP disappears (it is about 2.4\% more expensive). Planners should set service targets and penalties before reading the cost comparison.
\item \textbf{Freeze and log the configuration.} Treat the system prompt as a controlled file, log every call (input hash, time, session, raw response) and re-run before changing the prompt. Our reproducibility argument rests on this log, not on the platform's model identifier, which the client cannot see.
\item \textbf{Cost and latency.} Each LLM generation took several minutes, whereas the OR baseline takes milliseconds. The LLM is attractive where analyst time and not compute is scarce, but a deployment needs batching and monitoring. Periodic regeneration as demand and lead times change would also support adaptable planning under uncertainty \cite{sengupta2025uncertainty}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Research Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
In a three-plant industrial dataset, a prompt-configured LLM generates feasible, reviewable $(r,Q)$ policies for every material. In the simulation they cost less than ERP-derived parameters but more than history-only data-driven OR policies (Croston/SBA, empirical-quantile). We therefore do not present InventOR as a method that outperforms optimization; its claimed role is that of a reviewed candidate generator, and whether that role helps planners is a hypothesis that the simulation cannot test. The information-controlled evaluation is what supports this reading: against the ERP-informed arms alone, the LLM would appear much better ($-11.4\%$ against SAP and $-30.5\%$ against the SLT-informed OR in the hard-limit replay).

\paragraph{Design principles for LLM-based decision support.}
The evidence supports six principles that we derive from this application and offer as hypotheses for further design-science work \cite{hevner2004design}; they are not tested beyond it. \textbf{DP1, bound the generator:} give the LLM the role of candidate generator with a prompt that fixes the method, the constraints and the output schema, and no authority to act. \textbf{DP2, verify deterministically and along time:} check every output with code, and check feasibility along the trajectory, because point checks of constraints can pass while the constraints are violated over time (Section~\ref{subsec:capacity}). \textbf{DP3, show counterfactual evidence:} give the reviewer a simulated comparison with credible references, so that review is based on outcomes and not on the persuasiveness of the justification. \textbf{DP4, control information in evaluation:} compare against baselines that use the same information and against a baseline that uses none, and withhold incumbent inputs from the LLM in an ablation, otherwise a gain may merely reflect a weak benchmark (Sections~\ref{subsec:arms-results}, \ref{subsec:ablation}). \textbf{DP5, freeze and log:} treat the prompt as a controlled file, log every call and repeat the runs, because individual outputs vary (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}). \textbf{DP6, escalate by rule:} define in advance when proposals are trusted, escalated or rejected, to limit complacency \cite{parasuraman2010complacency} (Section~\ref{subsec:rules}).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(i) The data come from one manufacturer and three plants; generalization is not shown. (ii) The platform does not expose the model identifier or inference settings, so the model is operator-declared. (iii) No planner outcomes were measured. The dialog component is a prototype record, not an evaluated interface, and the claimed benefits of reviewability, auditability and planner support are design intentions that we did not test; a field study with planners is required to measure usability, workload, override behaviour and decision quality. (iv) The simulation uses a deterministic arrival at the rounded mean lead time, excludes observed receipts, corrections and scrap, and starts with an empty pipeline. Lead-time variability is not simulated, which may bias comparisons that depend on safety lead time (for example against the SLT-informed OR). We also did not report whether each arm attained the service target. (v) The primary results use a zero shortage penalty and standard holding and ordering costs; with a positive penalty the cost ranking changes. Costs are dominated by a few high-demand materials and the 346 pairs are not independent draws, so the bootstrap intervals understate uncertainty. (vi) The $(r,Q)$ policy is a single-item, single-echelon family; multi-item coupling and shared capacity are outside the scope. (vii) Run-to-run variation of individual policies is substantial, and Plant C required calendar reconstruction. (viii) The reference arms comprise ERP-derived and normal-approximation $(r,Q)$ policies, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile $(r,Q)$ policies and a simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$ policy; we did not include deep reinforcement-learning or neural forecasting baselines, so the comparison with the state of the art is incomplete. The ERP-derived arm is the ERP safety stock combined with our own order quantity and is not an observed record of the plants' actual practice. (ix) The trust, escalation and rejection rules of Section~\ref{subsec:rules} have not been evaluated.

\paragraph{Research perspectives.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Ensembling and consistency.} Aggregating several generations (median or consensus policies) may reduce the individual-policy noise while retaining the LLM's justification; the copilot findings for spare parts \cite{nakhost2025copilot} suggest this is promising.
\item \emph{Hybrid generators.} Combining the best data-driven OR policy (Croston/SBA or empirical quantile) with the LLM (the LLM reviewing and annotating the OR policy, or choosing among candidates by regime), as proposed for LLM--OR integration \cite{wasserkrug2025enhancing}, could capture the OR's cost level and the LLM's flags.
\item \emph{Comparison with trained methods.} A benchmark that compares prompt-based generators with deep reinforcement-learning and other trained policies \cite{gabellini2026novel, kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl} on identical data and information would clarify the trade-off between setup effort and policy quality.
\item \emph{Intermittent-demand and richer policy families.} Allowing order-up-to or $(s,S)$ policies and regime-specific estimators may help the lumpy and Plant C pairs \cite{prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}.
\item \emph{Planner-in-the-loop field trials.} Prospective studies should compare planners working with and without the aid on a matched set of materials, and measure planner time, override rates and realized service and inventory, which our simulation cannot, and should examine adoption drivers and barriers with mixed-methods designs \cite{venkatesh2023adoption, cannas2023aiscm, trifone2026agentic}.
\item \emph{Standards for information-controlled evaluation.} Studies of LLM-based decision support systems should report an ERP-independent baseline, an ablation that withholds the incumbent's inputs, trajectory-level feasibility, and run-to-run variation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We defined InventOR as a human-reviewed decision support system for a recurring production-planning decision, the setting of replenishment parameters per plant-material pair, and evaluated it against six design requirements on a three-plant industrial dataset. A prompt-configured LLM generated feasible, justified $(r,Q)$ policies for every material without training, and a deterministic gate, a hard-limit simulation and explicit review rules made these proposals checkable by a planner. The proposals cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at slightly lower service, but the LLM costs about 6\% more than a history-only OR policy, 8\% more than a Croston/SBA policy and 18\% more than an empirical-quantile policy; when lost sales are priced at one unit cost or more, ERP-derived parameters are the cheapest of the non-tuned arms and a simulation-tuned policy is cheapest overall. Storage limits that hold in a static check are exceeded along simulated trajectories for every method, and the LLM loses to ERP at one plant. The evidence therefore supports InventOR as a checkable candidate generator inside a reviewed process, with trajectory-level storage checks, explicit shortage pricing and a frozen, logged configuration. It does not show that the LLM out-optimizes a data-driven OR baseline, and the benefit to planners remains to be tested.

The design principles (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) are hypotheses for LLM-based decision support in settings where parameters are set item by item. Field evidence on planner behaviour and realized outcomes is the central open step.

\section{Formal Definitions}
\label{app:math}

\paragraph{Reference calculation.}
These are the standard textbook calculations \cite{silver2017inventory, axsater2015inventory}. For pair $i$, let $\bar d_i$ and $\sigma_{D,i}$ be the mean and standard deviation of pre-cutoff working-day demand, $L_i$ and $\sigma_{L,i}$ the mean and standard deviation of the lead time in working days, $K_i$ the ordering cost, $h_i$ the annual holding cost per unit (unit cost times holding rate), $z_i$ the standard normal quantile of the service target, and $250$ the working days per year. The order quantity is $Q_i=\max\{\mathrm{MOQ}_i,\lceil\sqrt{2K_i\cdot250\bar d_i/h_i}\,\rceil\}$. The lead-time-demand standard deviation is $\sigma_{LTD,i}=\sqrt{L_i\sigma_{D,i}^2+\bar d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2}$, the safety stock $SS_i=\lceil z_i\sigma_{LTD,i}\rceil$ and the reorder point $R_i=\lceil\bar d_iL_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+SS_i\rceil$, where $L^{\mathrm{eff}}_i$ is the mean lead time for the history-only OR and the mean lead time plus the ERP safety lead time for the SLT-informed OR and the ERP-floor OR. The SAP-derived arm uses the ERP safety stock and the same $Q_i$. All policies are projected to $SS_i+Q_i\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ when $\mathrm{MOQ}_i+SS_i$ fits.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
On each post-cutoff working day, demand is served from on-hand stock, unmet demand is lost, and an order of size $Q_i$ is placed when the inventory position (on-hand plus on order) is at or below $R_i$ and arrives after the rounded mean lead time in subsequent working observations. Observed receipts, corrections and scrap are ignored. The total cost is $TC_i=h_i\bar I_iT/250+K_iN_i$, where $\bar I_i$ is the mean on-hand stock over the $T$ simulated working days and $N_i$ the number of orders. Fill rate is served divided by demanded units. In the hard-limit replay an order is truncated to $\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ minus the inventory position, and no order is placed if the truncated quantity is zero.
