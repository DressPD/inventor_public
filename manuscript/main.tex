\begin{abstract}
How should training-free large language model (LLM) replenishment proposals be screened before operational consideration? \emph{InventOR} supplies auditable industrial evidence through deterministic cost, service and trajectory evaluation. For 365 plant-material pairs from three plants, three original generations, seven reference arms and an ERP-input ablation are evaluated on held-out recorded series. Three masked generations and rescaled originals share 338 common pairs, not independent datasets. On the original 346-pair common-feasible cohort, LLM policies cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters and 30--31\% less than code using ERP planning inputs, but 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile baselines. Mean fill is 0.1--0.4 percentage points below ERP-derived policies; volume-weighted fill is much lower than mean material fill. Plant C and priced shortages reverse the pooled ERP comparison. Static storage checks miss trajectory breaches, while capacity clipping releases sub-MOQ orders. A separately identified offline intervention reuses frozen policies with strict admission, supported by a conditional invariant and finite-state counterexamples, without guaranteeing service or optimality. Masked scoring supports numerical replay, not regeneration of proprietary inference. Source-demand semantics and additive grain remain unverified. The practical finding is comparator-aware screening with joint order-admission checks; measured planner benefits and operational deployment remain unevaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Manufacturing replenishment requires plant-material reorder points, safety stocks and order quantities. Revising them requires demand/lead-time estimation, inventory theory, minimum order quantity (MOQ) and storage checks, and planner explanations. LLMs with code interpreters are proposed for supply-chain decisions \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai, trifone2026agentic}, extending data analytics in logistics \cite{wang2016bigdata}; their numerical proposals still require independent evaluation.

Three problems limit its use. First, LLM outputs can be unfaithful or fabricated \cite{ji2023survey}, so a proposed policy is a candidate, not a decision. Second, superiority claims over ERP parameters are hard to interpret when the LLM and the comparators receive different information. Third, a storage rule that holds when a policy is written down can be violated as stock builds up over time.

The empirical problem is to distinguish an attractive ERP comparison from a proposal worth reviewing. InventOR evaluates training-free, fixed per-material $(r,Q)$ proposals against seven reference arms with explicit input boundaries and trajectory checks. It is not a new optimization algorithm or generator/verifier architecture: recent work already addresses mechanism-aware verification, simulation integration and inventory-policy evolution (Section~\ref{sec:literature}). An enterprise LLM receives a fixed prompt without project-specific training and proposes a policy, justification and escalation flag. Deterministic software parses, gates and simulates proposals; planner authority is a proposed deployment boundary (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

We study five questions on real industrial data: what cost, service and inventory the LLM policies achieve relative to ERP-derived parameters, data-driven baselines and textbook code, with explicit input boundaries (RQ1); how their differences relate descriptively to parameter similarity, run-to-run noise, demand segments and shortage prices (RQ2); whether policies respect storage limits along simulated trajectories (RQ3); how stable they are across repeated generations (RQ4); and which plant and demand-regime results might inform proposed review and escalation rules (RQ5).

We contribute (C1) comparator-aware industrial evidence for screening training-free proposals (Sections~\ref{sec:scope} and \ref{sec:aid}); (C2) seven reference arms, an ERP-input ablation and trajectory-level MOQ/storage diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:design}); (C3) frozen original and masked-policy replay with a matched six-run report, plus separately labeled strict-admission support (Section~\ref{sec:results}; Supplementary File S2); and (C4) conditional operational implications and testable next steps for planner evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

Against ERP-derived parameters the original LLM policies cost about 11\% less at mean fill 0.1--0.4 points lower, but cost 6\%, 8\% and 18\% more than history-only, Croston/SBA and empirical-quantile policies. Code using ERP inputs costs 44--45\% more than the LLM, showing the importance of input treatment. Median policy ratios resemble textbook code, and the six-run matched report preserves the ranking. Static-compliant policies exceed storage limits on unconstrained trajectories for 53--66\% of pairs, which motivates trajectory-level gating rather than a claim of operational feasibility.


\section{Problem Setting and Workflow}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{The decision problem}
\label{subsec:decision}
The decision supported is the periodic (re)setting of ERP reorder points, safety stocks and order quantities. This partly structured operational-control decision \cite{gorry1971framework} combines textbook calculation with judgement about estimators, intermittent demand, recorded lead times and constraints. The material planner decides; minimum order quantity (MOQ) and physical storage constrain proposals. The evaluated scope is single item, single echelon, daily working-observation review, lost sales and the $(r,Q)$ family. Live order release, supplier selection, forecasting-model development and multi-echelon networks are out of scope. The LLM proposes candidates; it does not place orders, decide deployment or run the deterministic held-out evaluation.

\subsection{The InventOR workflow and its level of automation}
\label{subsec:definition}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Definition.} \emph{InventOR is a proposed human-reviewed planning workflow for each plant-material pair: (i) a prompt-configured LLM proposes an $(r,Q)$ policy, justification and escalation flag; (ii) deterministic software gates it against explicit constraints; (iii) offline simulation compares it with reference policies on held-out recorded series; and (iv) a planner would review the result and alone decide ERP changes.}
\end{quote}
Only generation, deterministic gating and offline evaluation were executed; planner review was not studied. In the levels of automation of \textcite{parasuraman2000model}, the proposed workflow would operate at a low-to-intermediate level, leaving execution to humans. Section~\ref{subsec:rules} proposes safeguards against complacency and automation bias \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}.

\subsection{Requirements on the workflow}
\label{subsec:requirements}
The requirements follow from the known unreliability of LLM outputs \cite{ji2023survey}, guidance on human--AI interaction and accountability \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}, run-to-run nondeterminism of LLM inference \cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism} and the constraints of inventory control. Table~\ref{tab:requirements} ties each to a design feature (Section~\ref{sec:aid}) and an evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:literature}

\paragraph{Industrial inventory evidence and validation.}
\textcite{bai2012batch} study a practical batch-ordering policy under incomplete demand knowledge; \textcite{fu2019unison} link intermittent-demand forecasting to an electronics-distribution application. These motivate implementable comparators and explicit information boundaries, not the assumption that better forecasts ensure better policies. \textcite{lau2008effects} compare inventory policies under simulated operating conditions, supporting conditional cost/service interpretation. InventOR likewise distinguishes estimation, static feasibility and realized replay outcomes; its recorded-series, lost-sales replay is not a replication of those experiments.

\paragraph{Human--AI decision making in planning.}
Decision support aims to improve human judgement, not replace it \cite{gorry1971framework, sprague1980framework, shim2002past}; suggestion scope and verification influence appropriate reliance \cite{parasuraman2000model, parasuraman2010complacency}. Using a retail-company case and simulation, \textcite{wang2024aversion} document algorithm aversion in safety-stock decisions during disruptions and report better algorithmic performance in that setting. People also discount algorithms after seeing them err \cite{dietvorst2015aversion}. In a German retail-logistics case, \textcite{loske2021humanai} report efficiency advantages for two depots fully using AI routing under their DEA analyses. Neither finding establishes benefits from InventOR, which retains planner authority.

\paragraph{LLMs in operations and supply-chain decisions.}
\textcite{wu2026disruptions} compares automated and human-specified LLM-assisted inventory optimization under disruptions. \textcite{zabraoui2026conversational} address conversational inventory analytics with an EOQ module. \textcite{wang2025evolutionary} separate candidate generation from scheduling evaluation; \textcite{elbaz2026warehouse} combine agentic warehouse optimization and simulation. \textcite{yan2026mechanism} describe mechanism-aware multi-agent scheduling with generation--verification, and SimGPT integrates Simio with LLMs across simulation generation, execution and analysis \cite{dehghani2026simgpt}. InvEvolve's arXiv v4 preprint describes reinforcement-learning-trained LLMs and online inventory-policy evolution with confidence-based certification \cite{huang2026invevolve}. These are preview/abstract-supported descriptions, not independent verification of their methods or guarantees. InventOR instead evaluates fixed prompt-configured proposals without project-specific training; it does not benchmark or claim superiority over these systems.
Reviews map generative AI opportunities in supply chains \cite{jackson2024generative, fossowamba2023chatgpt, fossowamba2023ijpe, li2024genaiscm, song2026llmscm, panja2026genai} and emphasize human roles, data quality and governance \cite{trifone2026agentic, cannas2023aiscm, venkatesh2023adoption}. Examples include fine-tuned digital-manufacturing optimization \cite{amarasinghe2026finetuned}, learned order timing and quantity \cite{liu2026transformer}, and managerial decision biases \cite{chen2025manager}; these examples do not establish field-wide prevalence. Optimization modeling/code generation \cite{huang2025orlm, ahmaditeshnizi2024optimus, zhang2025or} differs from OptiGuide's natural-language explanation and what-if interaction with solver outcomes \cite{li2023large}, and from multi-agent inventory control \cite{quan2024invagent, jannelli2025agentic}. InventoryBench evaluates OR algorithms, LLM agents and humans on more than 1,000 instances, reporting benefits from OR-augmented LLMs \cite{baek2026inventorybench}. Our narrower question concerns fixed policies on an industrial extract. In a controlled retail-forecasting experiment with 123 human forecasters and five LLMs, \textcite{abolghasemi2025humans} find that the tested LLMs do not consistently outperform humans. \textcite{nakhost2025copilot} examine LLM-based spare-parts demand forecasting. We test policy variability directly (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}), rather than infer it from forecasting results.

\paragraph{Data-driven inventory control without LLMs.}
Learning-based replenishment includes non-stationary demand policies \cite{ren2024datadriven}, leading indicators \cite{sagaert2025leading}, deep reinforcement learning for omnichannel and multi-echelon systems \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl, ma2026omnichannel}, contextual robust optimization \cite{wang2026contextual} and forecasting-linked reinforcement learning \cite{schett2026rlorder}. For finite-horizon perishable inventory with non-stationary demand and zero lead time, \textcite{gulecyuz2025heuristic} compare a computationally efficient heuristic with optimal stochastic dynamic programming. \textcite{gabellini2026novel} evaluate joint replenishment, safety-stock and safety-lead-time decisions on real automotive-company data. Forecasting research links forecasts to safety stock \cite{beutel2012causal}, combined-error distributions \cite{barrow2016distributions} and inventory costs \cite{kourentzes2020optimising}. Model-based deep reinforcement learning addresses new products with short histories \cite{demizu2023inventory}; contextual inventory control is studied by \textcite{batsis2024contextual}. TFT-MPIR generates multi-period orders from historical/contextual inputs \cite{guo2025tftmpir}; graph recurrent networks address store-SKU replenishment \cite{ma2024retail}; RevIN-TSMixer connects forecasts to continuous-review inventory costs \cite{nguyen2026timemixer}. InventOR evaluates a fixed-prompt pretrained generator, not superiority over these trained methods.

\paragraph{Intermittent and spare-parts demand.}
Manufacturing histories are often intermittent or lumpy. Work began with the bias of Croston's estimator and its stock-control performance \cite{syntetos2001bias, syntetos2006stock} and continues with neural \cite{kourentzes2013neural}, bootstrapping \cite{hasni2019adjusted}, obsolescence-aware \cite{babai2019obsolescence}, temporal-hierarchy \cite{sanguri2023reconciliation} and state-space \cite{svetunkov2023iets} forecasts, order-up-to control \cite{rostamitabar2023orderupto}, safety-stock placement \cite{yazdani2023safetystock} and lead-time forecasting \cite{zhang2026leadtime}, all of which bear on the inputs of the policies we compare. The wider literature adds specialized estimators and classification schemes \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy, willemain2004new, prak2021robust, babai2026fifty}, spare-parts forecasting \cite{goldsmith2025spare} and multi-echelon spare-parts models \cite{caserta2026amazon}. InventOR is not a new inventory model and does not claim to beat trained or optimized methods; its $(r,Q)$ policy is the standard workhorse of continuous-review control \cite{hadley1963analysis, porteus2002foundations, silver2017inventory}.

\section{The InventOR Workflow}
\label{sec:aid}

\subsection{Architecture and components}
\label{subsec:architecture}
Following classical decision-support components \cite{sprague1980framework, shim2002past}, InventOR comprises \emph{data} (material-scoped histories, costs, constraints and ERP values; DR4); \emph{knowledge} (the frozen task, prohibited methods, rules and schema in Supplementary File S1; DR1, DR5); \emph{models} (a prompt-configured enterprise LLM/code-interpreter generator and a separate deterministic parser, gate and held-out simulator; DR1--DR3); \emph{dialog} (policy, justification, flag and comparison for proposed planner review; DR6); and \emph{governance} (input hashes, timestamps, sessions, raw responses and repeats; DR5). The generator is non-inspectable. The evaluated dialog is a per-pair output record; no interactive interface was built or evaluated.

\subsection{Operation in four decision steps}
For one material, InventOR uses four decision steps after input preparation; Figure~\ref{fig:tool_process_map} includes preparation as a fifth box.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figure_1_Workflow.pdf}
  \caption{InventOR workflow: material data are prepared, an LLM proposes a policy, and deterministic software parses, checks and simulates it. Planner review is a proposed deployment step, not an evaluated activity.}
  \label{fig:tool_process_map}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Generate.} The enterprise LLM/code interpreter receives one material's history and cost, constraint, service-target and ERP parameters (Section~\ref{subsec:data}). The written prompt is the only configuration; weights remain unchanged \cite{sahoo2024systematic}. It requests transparent statistics, a structured policy, justification and escalation flag, and gives an EOQ-related quantity rule with MOQ/storage bounds. It does not prescribe the normal-approximation safety-stock formula used by comparators. All scored artifacts resolve to fixed $(r,Q)$ policies; permitted family labels are not separate evaluated methods.
\item \textbf{Gate.} Deterministic code extracts the policy, rejects malformed or infeasible outputs (missing or non-numeric fields, non-positive reorder point or order quantity, negative safety stock, reorder point below safety stock, safety stock plus order quantity above the storage limit) and retries the generation up to three times. The backtest applies one further admission check, that the order quantity is not below the MOQ.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Each accepted policy is replayed on the material's demand observed \emph{after} the data cutoff, a held-out evaluation in the sense of out-of-sample forecast testing \cite{tashman2000out, hewamalage2023forecast}, together with the comparator policies, producing holding and ordering cost, fill rate and stockout days.
\item \textbf{Review (proposed deployment step).} A planner would inspect the policy, justification, escalation flag and simulated comparison and would retain sole authority over ERP changes. Recording the planner's decision, review time and comments is proposed future functionality; neither this logging nor planner outcomes was evaluated in the present study.
\end{enumerate}

The LLM is a \emph{candidate generator} whose output is gated; it does not run the study's deterministic held-out evaluation, place orders or decide ERP changes. The prompt prohibits Monte Carlo simulation, distribution fitting, machine-learning libraries and specialized intermittent-demand estimators, so that the policy rests on transparent statistics.

\subsection{Trust, review and reject rules}
\label{subsec:rules}
We propose three rules for planners, in line with guidelines for human--AI interaction and AI auditing \cite{amershi2019guidelines, raji2020accountability}. The rules and the 0.5-point threshold are design proposals not evaluated with planners; the trust rule would be met by a minority of pairs (the LLM is cheaper than the history-only OR in 74--77 of 346).
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*]
\item[Trust (consider after review)] only after checking both order-level MOQ and storage compliance, as well as a simulated cost no higher than the best history-only reference (Croston/SBA or empirical quantile) with fill not more than about 0.5 points lower. Capacity-clipped replay alone cannot establish the joint compliance needed to apply this rule.
\item[Escalate] when the LLM raises an escalation flag, the material is lumpy or has strongly seasonal or trending demand, the plant or calendar data were repaired, or the policy differs strongly between repeated generations.
\item[Reject] when the proposal fails the gate after the retry budget, exceeds the storage limit in the hard-limit replay, or is dominated (higher cost and lower fill) by a history-only reference.
\end{description}

\subsection{Provenance and reproducibility of the configuration}
\label{subsec:provenance}
The system prompt, deployed unchanged, defines the task, forbidden methods, capacity rules and output schema (Supplementary File S1). Calls log input SHA-256, UTC time, session identifier and raw response. The platform does not expose model identifier or sampling settings; Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6 with a code interpreter is operator-declared. Frozen policies support numerical replay, not regeneration of proprietary inference. Three repeated generations examine within-panel variation (Section~\ref{subsec:stability}).

\section{Application and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Evaluation approach}
\label{subsec:evalapproach}
The evaluation addresses Table~\ref{tab:requirements} through hard-limit replay with working-day receipts and no shortage penalty, plus sensitivities. This ex post simulation replays a processed operational-extract series; its source-demand definition and additive grain have not been validated. There are no live users. The history-only, SLT-informed and ERP-floor OR arms implement textbook EOQ and normal-approximation policies with different ERP-input treatments, not exact execution of a prescribed prompt formula. Outcomes characterize conditional policy cost, service and constraint failures, not usability or planner benefits.

\subsection{Industrial application and data}\label{subsec:data}
The data come from a large manufacturer, and the evaluation is retrospective. The dataset contains 233{,}078 daily records for 411 plant-material pairs from three plants (A, B and C), with a data cutoff on 10 March 2026 and a post-cutoff horizon observed to 27 August 2026. Eligibility requires at least 20 working-day observations before the cutoff and 40 on or after it within the evaluation horizon. Of the 411 pairs, 365 are eligible for scoring (234 in Plant A, 65 in Plant B, 66 in Plant C) and are sent to the LLM. For 19 pairs (all in Plant A), at least one of the SAP-derived or SLT-informed reference policies cannot be projected to satisfy MOQ and the static $SS+Q$ capacity rule while retaining its safety stock; these are excluded from outcome comparison, leaving a \emph{common-feasible cohort} of 346 pairs (215, 65 and 66). This is a comparator-specific exclusion, not proof of general policy infeasibility. Demand is concentrated (the five largest materials account for 40.3\% of demand). The source working-day flag of Plant C is all zero; we reconstruct it from the same-date majority of Plants A and B and test this repair in Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}. Data quality is a known threat to decision support \cite{watts2009data} and inventory records \cite{dehoratius2008inventory, kang2005information, mersereau2013information}.

The extract has 220{,}017 distinct plant-material/date groups. A masked-panel diagnostic finds 12{,}036 repeated groups across 62 of the full panel's 411 pairs: daily quantities repeat unchanged while context varies. Frozen scoring sums signed same-date demand before taking its absolute value, averages positive lead times and retains the last row's other attributes; stock is not summed. Without a source dictionary and extract-grain reconciliation, these are reproducible processing choices, not verified aggregation of distinct demand events. The implemented additive grain, sign handling, context selection and stock timing require source validation. The implementation is in \texttt{inventory\_llm/core.py}: \texttt{load\_grouped\_rows}, \texttt{demand}, \texttt{compute\_stats} and \texttt{eligible\_item\_keys}.

Each pair is sent as a material-scoped CSV with daily demand, recorded lead times, a working-day flag, the minimum order quantity, unit cost, holding-cost rate, ordering cost, service-level target, the storage limit, and two \emph{ERP planning parameters} (current safety stock and safety lead time), which are withheld in the ablation arm. The CSV also carries supplier and lane context fields that the prompt does not use. A masked version of the panel is released for replication (Section~\ref{subsec:masked}); there the plants are labelled PL1, PL2 and PL3 (corresponding to A, B and C).

The LLM and the classical comparators therefore do not necessarily use the same information, and we construct arms that make it explicit (Table~\ref{tab:arms}).

Three further arms use no ERP planning inputs. \emph{Croston/SBA OR} uses bias-corrected Croston demand \cite{croston1972forecasting, syntetos2005accuracy}, smoothing 0.1, and root mean squared one-step forecast error as demand spread, a study-specific heuristic rather than a validated lead-time-demand distribution. \emph{Empirical-quantile OR} uses the service-target quantile of rolling demand over rounded mean lead time. \emph{Simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$} searches reserve factors $-0.5,-0.25,\ldots,3.5$ and quantity multiples $0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2$ (up to 85 candidates), selecting the cheapest meeting the service target, or the best fill if none meets it. Statistics use all pre-cutoff data; tuning replays the last 250 working days without capacity clipping, initialized at each candidate's $R+Q$ with an empty pipeline. This is in-sample tuning, not nested validation; evaluation instead uses shared observed opening stock and capacity clipping. No post-cutoff demand informs selection. Deep reinforcement learning \cite{stranieri2024drl, kolyaei2025rl} is not benchmarked.

\subsection{Simulation, capacity replay and metrics}
Each pair is replayed as a single-item, single-echelon $(r,Q)$ system \cite{hadley1963analysis, axsater2015inventory, zipkin2000foundations, silver2017inventory, porteus2002foundations} with lost sales \cite{bijvank2011lost}, discrete working-day review, shared observed opening stock, an empty pipeline and deterministic mean-lead-time arrivals (\ref{app:math}). This is simulation, not reconstruction of actual orders \cite{grasso1984simulation}. Fill and shortage cost condition on the processed recorded series: availability-independent requests versus availability-influenced fulfilled issues is unverified; latent demand and historical lost sales are not reconstructed. Policy quantities satisfy MOQ \cite{heese2025moq}, but capacity-clipped orders \cite{zhang2023replenishment} may not.

The fixed finite-panel estimand conditions on recorded inputs and frozen policies. Costs use pair-specific horizons without exposure normalization; cost/fill means give equal weight per pair, not per plant. Volume-weighted fill is total served divided by total recorded units, undefined at zero total. Stockout days are summed. Contrasts use ratios of mean costs, not means of pairwise percentages. Intervals for paired mean cost/fill differences use a 5{,}000-resample percentile-bootstrap of pairs (95\%; seed 20261006): descriptive pair-composition sensitivity, not model-generation uncertainty or population inference; within-plant dependence is not modeled.

A static check $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$ does not show that storage limits hold as inventory evolves, because the reorder point includes lead-time demand and inventory on order can exceed the limit on arrival. We therefore record the post-receipt on-hand stock for every policy and day and report the share of pairs and days above the limit (the \emph{unconstrained} simulation), and we \emph{replay} every policy with each order truncated to the storage limit minus the current inventory position (the \emph{hard-limit replay}), which is our primary evaluation.

\subsection{Repeated runs and sensitivities}
To examine run-to-run variation, the entire cohort is generated three times (runs 1, 2 and 3) with the same prompt file and the same data. Run~1 serves as the reference for tabulated results unless stated otherwise; runs 2 and 3 provide replicates. Sensitivity analyses vary the working-day reconstruction (majority, any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C and calendar-day arrivals) and the shortage penalty (zero, one and five times unit cost).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Completion and policy characteristics}\label{subsec:completion}
All four original-data runs (three full-input and one ablation, Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}) return an $(r,Q)$ policy satisfying the numerical and static storage gate for all 365 pairs after at most three attempts per pair. The validity gate rejects non-positive reorder points and order quantities, negative safety stocks and reorder points below the safety stock. The ERP safety stock is zero for 198 of the 365 pairs (188 of the 346 in the cohort below), so for a majority of pairs the SAP-derived arm holds no safety stock at all.

\subsection{Headline comparison with ERP-derived parameters}\label{subsec:headline}

Table~\ref{tab:backtest-results} compares LLM cost using the reference-arm mean as denominator (positive: LLM costs more). Paired mean cost difference is $-377$ (95\% interval $-651$ to $-154$). LLM is cheaper in 241 pairs, simultaneously no costlier and no worse in fill in 226. Stockout days increase (910 versus 804). Volume-weighted fill is 78.7--79.3\% except for simulation-tuned (84.8\%), versus mean fill near 98\%: unpriced lost sales leave high-volume service low; costs are not compared at equal service.

Relative to SAP-derived parameters the LLM carries about 17\% less average inventory (16{,}256 versus 19{,}565 units) and about 16\% lower holding cost at about the same mean number of orders per material over its evaluation horizon (17.3 versus 17.6), and it accepts 11--20\% more stockout days (runs 3 and 2). The saving is therefore a trade of inventory for service, not a better ordering rhythm, and Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity} shows that once shortages carry a cost this trade no longer favours the LLM.

\subsection{Information asymmetry: what the LLM adds beyond the ERP inputs}
\label{subsec:arms-results}

First, the SLT-informed OR and its ERP-floor variant are the most expensive arms (4{,}211 and 4{,}248): the ERP safety lead time inflates their effective lead time and buffers. Against them the LLM is 30--31\% cheaper in every run, which mostly measures how heavily ERP inputs over-buffer.

Second, the \emph{history-only OR}, which uses no ERP inputs, costs 2{,}764 at a fill of 98.14\%. The LLM costs 5.9--6.0\% more in all three runs (run~1: $+164$, interval $+91$ to $+240$), with fill differences of $-0.03$, $-0.25$ and $+0.01$ points whose intervals include zero, and is cheaper in only 74--77 of 346 pairs. The stronger data-driven arms widen the gap: the LLM costs 8.2--8.4\% more than the Croston/SBA OR (2{,}705, fill 98.30\%; run~1: $+223$, interval $+97$ to $+362$; cheaper in 91--99 pairs) and 17.7--17.8\% more than the empirical-quantile OR (2{,}489, fill 98.35\%; $+439$, interval $+329$ to $+557$; cheaper in 44--49 pairs; mean fill 0.24 points lower). Part of the LLM's advantage over ERP-derived parameters therefore reflects weak ERP benchmarks.

The simulation-tuned $(r,Q)$ policy sits at a different operating point: an aggregate fill of 84.8\% and the lowest inventory (11{,}914 units) but a mean fill of 96.05\% and 1{,}973 stockout days, because it is tuned on the last 250 pre-cutoff working days. The LLM costs 10.3--10.4\% less at zero penalty with a 2.1-point higher mean fill, but the interval includes zero ($-2{,}527$ to $+857$); once shortages are priced the tuned policy is by far the cheapest arm (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{LLM without ERP planning parameters (ablation)}
\label{subsec:ablation}
This ablation blanks ERP safety stock and safety lead time, marking them ``not supplied'', with the same prompt and other inputs. All 365 original pairs returned a static-compliant policy. Median parameter ratios to run~1 are 1.00, but reorder points differ by more than 10\% in 54.6\% of pairs, against 48.3\% and 48.0\% between full-input runs. Hard-limit cost is 3{,}014 at mean fill 98.06\%: 8.8\% below SAP-derived (interval $-566$ to $-61$), but 9.0\% above History-only (interval $+134$ to $+387$), 11.4\% above Croston/SBA and 21.1\% above empirical-quantile. Relative to SAP-derived, costs are $-10.8\%$, $-21.9\%$ and $+41.6\%$ in Plants A/B/C. The Plant C reversal therefore persists without these fields; one stochastic ablation does not isolate their causal effect or prove that the LLM ignores them. Costs, MOQ, service target and capacity remain available to all arms.

\subsection{Storage limits along simulated trajectories}
\label{subsec:capacity}
All 365 original LLM policies satisfy $SS+Q\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}$, yet unconstrained post-receipt stock exceeds the limit for 183 pairs (52.9\%; 178--183 across runs; 10.2\% of pair-days) for the LLM, 198 (57.2\%) for SAP-derived and 230 (66.5\%) for SLT-informed OR. The static rule does not bound pipeline accumulation and is insufficient for trajectory feasibility. In the hard-limit replay, clipping keeps every arm within one unit of the limit (integer truncation of inventory position), but clipped orders may fall below the MOQ. Enforcing capacity raises LLM mean cost from 2{,}850 to 2{,}928 and SAP-derived from 3{,}184 to 3{,}305 without changing the ranking.

\subsection{Plants and demand regimes}
\label{subsec:plants}
The average hides a strong plant effect. In original run~1 the LLM costs 14.1\% less than SAP-derived in Plant A (cheaper in 179 of 215 pairs) and 22.7\% less in Plant B (54 of 65), but 36.2\% \emph{more} in Plant C (cheaper in 8 of 66); the Plant~C loss is similar in the other full runs ($+32.2\%$, $+31.5\%$) and persists under every working-day reconstruction in which Plant C can be scored (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}). Figure~\ref{fig:segments} separately shows all six runs on the matched masked-scale cohort. Against the data-driven baselines the original run~1 LLM costs more in every plant: 7.2\%, 3.6\% and 6.7\% more than the history-only OR, 13.1\%, 2.3\% and 6.2\% more than the Croston/SBA OR, and 20.8\%, 7.7\% and 29.5\% more than the empirical-quantile OR in Plants A, B and C. The SLT-informed and history-only ORs coincide in Plant C because ERP safety lead time is zero for all its pairs.

By demand regime, a study-specific high-variability proxy (pre-cutoff working-day demand coefficient of variation $\mathrm{CV}\geq1$) identifies 33 materials in the 346-pair original cohort. Labelled ``lumpy'' in the evidence code, this is not the conventional ADI/$\mathrm{CV}^2$ taxonomy. They show the largest reduction against SAP-derived ($-25.4\%$ in run~1), but the history-only and Croston/SBA ORs cost about 9--10\% less than the LLM there; with 33 materials this is indicative only.

\subsection{Descriptive comparison with formula-based code}\label{subsec:mechanism}
The deterministic History-only OR uses textbook EOQ with an MOQ floor and normal-approximation safety stock (\ref{app:math}); the SLT-informed and ERP-floor ORs additionally use ERP inputs available to the LLM (Section~\ref{sec:design}). The historical prompt constrains the economic quantity and permits several policy families; it does not prescribe these comparator formulas in full. This is a descriptive comparison with reference code, not a test of compliance with a prescribed normal-approximation recipe.

\paragraph{The LLM resembles the formula-based policies.}
The median smoothed ratio $(x_{\mathrm{LLM}}+1)/(x_{\mathrm{code}}+1)$ is 1.00 for order quantity (medians 2{,}810 and 2{,}828 units) and safety stock (712 and 718, against an ERP median of 0); LLM parameters are averaged over the three original runs first. Here ``code'' denotes History-only OR. The corresponding reorder-point ratio is 1.09, including across the segments in Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}, where LLM cost is 4--9\% higher. These summaries indicate similarity and a reorder-point uplift, not item-level proof that the LLM executes the formulas faithfully.

\paragraph{Input treatment changes the ranking against code.}
Code that receives the same ERP inputs as the LLM costs 4{,}211 (SLT-informed OR) and 4{,}248 (ERP-floor OR) per pair against 2{,}928 for the LLM in run~1, i.e.\ 44--45\% more using the LLM mean as denominator, because ERP safety lead times inflate the textbook reorder point. Code ignoring ERP planning inputs costs 2{,}764; the LLM costs 5.9\% more using that reference mean. The LLM resembles History-only rather than ERP-inflated code, consistent with Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Run-to-run noise.}
The within-pair cost range across the three original runs, divided by that pair's mean and averaged across pairs, is 10.5\%; this is not a standard deviation. The runs disagree on beating History-only code for 34.1\% of pairs. The LLM mean is cheaper than History-only for 17.1\% of pairs and SAP-derived for 76.3\%. Selecting each pair's best run with hindsight gives mean cost 2{,}765 (History-only: 2{,}764); selecting the worst gives 3{,}146. Such selection is not an implementable out-of-sample policy.

\paragraph{Plant C and segments.}
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} shows that the LLM advantage over SAP-derived parameters disappears in storage-bound pairs ($+4.9\%$ against $-14.9\%$ elsewhere) and reverses in Plant C ($+33.3\%$); it does not vary monotonically with demand variability. Plant C is not an LLM-specific failure: the History-only code is also 28\% more costly than SAP-derived parameters there, so the ERP parameters are leaner than any formula-based policy in that plant when lost sales are not charged. Plant C also has the least regular demand (median coefficient of variation of daily demand 0.61, lead-time coefficient of variation 0.78, against 0.51 and 0.63 over all pairs).

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{subsec:sensitivity}
\paragraph{Plant C calendar.} Plant C's working-day flag required reconstruction, so we re-scored all policies under four alternatives (any-plant, strict-all, exclusion of Plant C, calendar-day arrivals). Where Plant C can be scored, the LLM remains 31--35\% more expensive than SAP-derived there, so the Plant C result is not an artefact of the repair.

\paragraph{Shortage penalty.} Rescoring the original fixed trajectories at one and five times unit cost makes the simulation-tuned policy about 33\% cheaper than SAP-derived; LLM run~1 costs 2.4\% more than SAP-derived at both multiples. These are accounting sensitivities, not policy optimization at each penalty. Table~\ref{tab:breakeven} retains the original-scale analysis; the public six-run evidence also records shortage exposure for masked-scale rescoring.

\paragraph{Break-even shortage price.}
Table~\ref{tab:breakeven} re-scores the same replays for a lost-sales penalty between zero and one unit cost per unit. The LLM is cheaper than SAP-derived parameters only while the penalty stays below 0.9\% of the unit cost, and cheaper than the History-only and empirical-quantile code only above 1.5\% and 2.4\%, by about 1\% or less. \paragraph{Secondary strict-admission validation.} Supplementary File S2 separately replays the same 338 pairs after reproducing frozen clipping results. Fixed policies use floored physical headroom and defer releases below MOQ. \section{Discussion, Limitations and Research Perspectives}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the evidence shows.}
Cost/service/trajectory screening distinguishes ERP-relative savings from competitive proposals. All original $(r,Q)$ proposals passed the static gate; masked generations include missing and invalid outputs. Policy similarity and run variation characterize outcomes, not internal calculations. S2 makes a narrow admission-rule intervention inspectable without rewriting the original experiment. Neither that intervention nor input matching establishes equal-service superiority.

\paragraph{Implications for practice.}
(1) Benchmark against data-driven code, not ERP alone; input treatment matters. (2) Price shortages before interpreting cost rankings (Section~\ref{subsec:sensitivity}). (3) Verify storage and MOQ jointly: static compliance and capacity clipping do not ensure both. (4) Expect plant differences; define review/escalation rules \cite{parasuraman2010complacency}. (5) Version prompts, retain provenance and repeat generations. (6) No project-specific training was needed, but measured runtime, model fees, engineering effort and planner time were not collected; low configuration burden is not evidence of fast or cheap deployment.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The evidence comes from one manufacturer's three-plant panel, which the masked generations reuse; source-demand and data-grain validation remains outstanding: repeated context rows leave the additive grain unverified, and the recorded series need not reflect availability-independent requests. The model identity is operator-declared, planner outcomes were not measured, and fixed-policy comparisons are not equal-service; separately retuned arms do not match held-out service. Policies are single-item $(r,Q)$, and trained neural or reinforcement-learning baselines are left to future work.

\paragraph{Research perspectives.}
(1) \emph{Constraint-aware hybrids:} test mechanism-aware generation with independent joint-feasibility checks, motivated by Yan--Ji and SimGPT, without transferring performance claims. Policy aggregation is untested here, distinct from spare-parts forecasting \cite{nakhost2025copilot}; LLM review of data-driven policies \cite{wasserkrug2025enhancing} also needs evaluation. (2) \emph{Matched comparisons:} compare InvEvolve, trained inventory-control frameworks \cite{gabellini2026novel}, reinforcement learning \cite{kolyaei2025rl, stranieri2024drl} and richer $(s,S)$ policies \cite{prak2021robust, babai2026fifty} at matched information, service and measured setup/inference/review budgets. (3) \emph{Planner trials:} measure time, overrides, realized service/inventory and adoption \cite{venkatesh2023adoption, cannas2023aiscm, trifone2026agentic} prospectively; published guarantees elsewhere do not transfer to InventOR.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
InventOR provides auditable screening evidence for training-free replenishment proposals on 365 industrial plant-material pairs. Original policies cost about 11\% less than ERP-derived parameters at slightly lower mean service, but classical baselines remain cheaper, Plant C reverses the pooled ERP ranking and shortage pricing changes the conclusion. Six-run replay preserves aggregate rankings on one 338-pair panel, not independent datasets. Static checks and capacity clipping miss joint trajectory constraints; S2 supplies separate, conditional admission-rule support rather than a service guarantee. Comparator-aware cost/service screening and explicit order-admission checks are the practical result. Source-data validation, prospective planner studies and external validation remain necessary.


\appendix
\section{Formal Definitions}
\label{app:math}

\paragraph{Reference calculation.}
These are standard textbook calculations \cite{silver2017inventory, axsater2015inventory}. For pair $i$, $\bar d_i,\sigma_{D,i}$ describe pre-cutoff working-day demand; $L_i,\sigma_{L,i}$ describe recorded lead times; $K_i$ is ordering cost, $h_i$ annual holding cost per unit, and $z_i$ the normal service-target quantile. Set $Q_i=\max\{\mathrm{MOQ}_i,\lceil\sqrt{2K_i\cdot250\bar d_i/h_i}\,\rceil\}$, $\sigma_{LTD,i}=\sqrt{L_i^{\mathrm{eff}}\sigma_{D,i}^2+\bar d_i^2\sigma_{L,i}^2}$, $SS_i=\lceil z_i\sigma_{LTD,i}\rceil$ and $R_i=\lceil\bar d_iL_i^{\mathrm{eff}}+SS_i\rceil$. Here $L_i^{\mathrm{eff}}=L_i$ for History-only and $L_i+\max(\mathrm{ERP\ SLT}_i,0)$ for SLT-informed and ERP-floor; the latter also floors $SS_i$ at ERP safety stock. SAP-derived uses ERP safety stock, the same $Q_i$, and ERP safety lead time in its reorder point. Projection enforces $SS_i+Q_i\le\texttt{max\_storage\_units}_i$ when MOQ plus safety stock fits.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
Review is discrete, not event-driven continuous review. Each post-cutoff working observation receives due orders, serves demand, loses unmet demand, then orders at most once if position (on hand plus on order) $\le R_i$. Rounded mean lead time is at least one subsequent working observation. Historical receipts/corrections/scrap are ignored. Cost is $TC_i=h_i\bar I_iT/250+K_iN_i$: $\bar I_i$ averages end-observation stock over $T$ working observations; $N_i$ counts placed orders, including arrivals beyond the horizon; no terminal salvage is applied. Fill is served/recorded units (100\% for zero recorded demand); stockout days count unmet-demand working observations. Positive hard-limit orders are $\min\{Q_i,\mathrm{capacity}_i-\lfloor\mathrm{position}_i\rfloor\}$, even below MOQ; otherwise none. Observed opening stock is not clipped.
